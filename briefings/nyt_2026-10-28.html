NYT Daily Briefing

Israel votes at record rates, no winner · Lula's ten-million-person digital army · a three-day truce for a city 92 percent empty · Serbia's winners have no leader, by design · the hemisphere shifts without Washington

Wednesday, October 28, 2026

Cluster 1: Geopolitics & International Affairs

Thematic Hook

Four countries, four substitutes for a transmission belt that has stopped turning.

Israel votes at record rates and produces no winner (#4)

Turnout reached 77.7 percent, the highest since 1999, with Arab-majority towns exceeding their 2022 totals. Exit polls put Arab parties at 17 or 18 seats — their best showing ever — and showed neither bloc with a majority. Likud held a small lead over Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar, and coalition formation is expected to take "weeks or even months." Culture Minister Miki Zohar warned that the public "will not forgive nor forget" any alliance with Arab parties, which is a strategic statement: it is an attempt to raise the price of the decisive move before arithmetic forces it. Bret Stephens' four observations (#32) are worth reading against the result — the vote says more about exhaustion with the current coalition than about enthusiasm for what replaces it.

Lula's ten-million-person digital army (#5, #6)

Lula became the first candidate in Brazilian history to win a presidential race after finishing second in the first round, converting a first-round deficit into roughly double his 2022 margin. The engine was a WhatsApp network that grew from a few thousand followers to ten million people in a country of 214 million. Political scientist Christian Lynch called it "a very big victory for global democracy," driven by mobilization at a scale Brazil had not seen. The strategic consequence is larger than Brazil: Lula is now the only leader in Latin America, and one of the few anywhere, who has defied Trump and won.

The loser's flight, and Washington's role in it (#6)

Flávio Bolsonaro left Brazil after federal police asked for his passports, crossing into Argentina by land and boarding a private flight to Guayaquil. Three US officials say he is heading for the United States, and the State Department plans to issue visas for him and his family. Brazil's Supreme Court had ordered the passports surrendered within 24 hours. Granting entry to a man under investigation for links to a scheme that allegedly laundered more than $1 billion signals to every capital in the hemisphere that US alignment is a personal guarantee rather than an institutional one.

A nine-ton helicopter versus an eleven-foot drone (#7)

The Army is training Apache crews at Grafenwöhr to shoot down drones, a mission the helicopter was never designed for. In a drone-dominated battlefield the Apache's natural prey — tanks and armor — has largely disappeared. The exercise matters because Iranian Shaheds have "slipped through American air defenses" and were responsible for almost all American deaths in the war with Iran; in June, one took down an Apache over the Strait of Hormuz. This is a production-cost problem being treated as a tactics problem. A Shahed costs a fraction of anything fired at it.

A three-day truce for a city that is 92 percent empty (#8)

A local cease-fire brokered by Melania Trump and urged by Pope Leo XIV, three days after his cancer surgery, took effect Tuesday in Oleshky. Roughly 2,000 civilians remain of the 24,000 who lived there before the 2022 invasion. By Monday midday a volunteer had collected 200 names. "They gave us so little time," she said. The constraint is logistics, not willingness — residents kept asking whether others had signed up before joining, which means the information cascade never had room to run.

Serbia's winners have no leader, by design (#10)

Aleksandar Vucic was defeated on Sunday and the student-selected Student List won at least 140 of 250 seats. During nearly two years of protest the movement deliberately refused to name a leader, "fearing that doing so would allow the authorities to undermine their cause by making targeted arrests" — and the discipline worked. Now a state television anchor has apologized on air for years of "one-sided information," a tabloid editor's office was stormed, and nobody knows who will be prime minister. Cardiologist Ilija Srdanovic pleaded with supporters: "Please, do not resort to any form of violence. We must remain dignified until the very end." He is the first name on the slate, not the leader, and has no authority to enforce what he asks.

The hemisphere shifts without Washington (#9, #12)

María Corina Machado returned to Caracas after nearly a year in exile, to crowds that lined up along her motorcade, without US help and without US obstruction. Former ambassador James B. Story drew the distinction cleanly: "Delcy derives her legitimacy solely from the White House, whereas Maria Corina derives hers from the Venezuelan people." Meanwhile King Charles III opened a seven-day Caribbean tour by declining to apologize for slavery while acknowledging "the great suffering of the past" — a monarchy managing the residue of an empire it can no longer command.

Elsewhere (#11, #13, #14, #15)

Almost half of House Democrats voted to cut off aid to Israel in the past year, and the party now treats the issue as a major fault line for next year (#11). Jordan struck inside Syria, saying it targeted Islamic State (#13). British prosecutors said an incident at RAF Fairford, a US-used base, was coordinated by a man acting for Iran (#14). An Egyptian court ordered the independent news site Mada Masr closed (#15).

Prediction

Israel will not have a sworn government within four weeks of the vote. (Rationale: neither bloc holds a majority, Arab parties at 17–18 seats are the pivotal quantity, and Zohar's public warning indicates the price of that partnership is being deliberately escalated — 2019–2022 produced four elections in under four years on similar arithmetic.) Confidence: 80% (high confidence)

Cluster 2: US Politics & Domestic Policy

Thematic Hook

A seven-day clock is being used to make procedure look like obstruction.

The flood-the-zone campaign (#1)

Attorney General Todd Blanche's department unsealed new charges against James Comey on Monday — the third criminal case against him — after indicting a Virginia Democrat on drug-trafficking charges and suing to pre-empt certification of Alaska's Senate race. Greg Rosen, a former federal prosecutor, named the method: "This is a classic flood-the-zone strategy — run so many plays at once that the defense doesn't know where to look." The most revealing detail is in the paperwork. Court filings bear "typos, unorthodox tactics and the signatures of the department's senior political leadership" — meaning the career verification layer that normally warrants a filing has been bypassed. Department spokeswoman Emily Covington says the department follows "the facts and the law, not anyone's political timetable."

The courts say not yet (#17, #16)

A Ninth Circuit panel refused, 2-1, to let the administration leapfrog a district judge and order California to set aside as many as 20,000 mail ballots. Judge Mark J. Bennett, a Trump appointee, joined Judge Gabriel P. Sanchez in holding that the Justice Department "failed to allow" the lower court "adequate opportunity" to consider the case; a hearing was already scheduled for Tuesday. The Supreme Court took the opposite approach on a different matter, allowing the Texas–DOJ deportation agreement to stand in a one-sentence order with no reasoning and no noted dissents.

Election officials are preparing for the federal government (#2)

"This is the first time ever that we have had to engage in scenario planning to safeguard against potential federal interference in elections," said Shenna Bellows, Maine's secretary of state. "It seems like the federal government is now adversarial to the states." Officials are drilling poll workers on the statute barring federal agents from polling places and on the limits of federal monitors' authority. Whether any scenario materializes is unknown. That fifty state and local administrations have concluded it might, and are drilling for it, is itself the finding.

The federal net widens through fish and wildlife officers (#18)

In Florida, officers from the Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission detained an angler over a bucket of egg-bearing blue crabs and then summoned immigration agents, who took him away. The 287(g) program now deputizes state agriculture departments, gaming commissions, fish and wildlife agencies and alcohol control boards — agencies with no immigration experience and, critics say, a heightened risk of misconduct.

Mississippi is competitive (#20)

Trump and Kamala Harris have both campaigned in a state Democrats have not won in decades. Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith faces district attorney Scott Colom, and Trump's rally pitch — "I'm here because Cindy is doing great. But I've just got to make sure" — is not the language of a safe seat. Mississippi Republican Party chairman Mike Hurst is unmoved: "We've seen this movie before."

The number that matters (#3)

Latinos now favor Democrats by 30 percentage points, per the latest Times/Siena poll of 458 Hispanic voters taken Oct. 12–19. That reverses the 2024 inroads that many read as a durable realignment. Hispanic voters are at least 20 percent of registered voters in nearly two dozen competitive House districts across Arizona, California, Colorado, Florida, Pennsylvania and Texas. Whatever else happens on Nov. 3, the coalition the administration built two years ago is not consolidating.

Elsewhere (#21, #22, #23, #24, #33, #47)

The data center industry is spending to boost Darline Graham in South Carolina's Senate race, making AI infrastructure a direct force in state politics (#21). The Alexander brothers were sentenced to 30 years each after 12 victim statements, 11 delivered in person (#22). A Trump-aligned incumbent is suddenly at risk in an upstate New York district long considered safe (#23). A Brooklyn woman was convicted of training with ISIS in Syria (#24). Readers writing on the midterms ask whether the country wants "education, innovation and enlightened leadership" or "a personality cult and censorship" (#33). And in an Insider piece, a source who described an O.J. Simpson accomplice recanted after publication (#47).

Prediction

The Justice Department will announce or unseal at least one additional action against a named Trump adversary before Nov. 3. (Rationale: five such actions in the past ten days, an explicit pattern Rosen identifies as saturation tactics, and no observable internal constraint — the political layer is signing directly.) Confidence: 72% (high confidence)

Cluster 3: Business, Tech & Economy

Thematic Hook

Two governments are buying the price while leaving the price-setter alone — and a third set of institutions is collecting fees on the way through.

The Fed will hold, and the reason is on the calendar (#25)

Rates sit at 3.75 to 4 percent after September's unanimous quarter-point increase, inflation is broadening across goods and services, energy is elevated by the Iran war, and an AI-driven spending splurge shows "little sign of abating." Yet Kevin Warsh is expected to hold on Wednesday, days before an election. Loretta Mester, who ran the Cleveland Fed for a decade, asks the only question that matters: "The economics support another rate increase. By not moving in October, I'm a little skeptical whether a timely path is substantially different from just being on a downward path to 2 percent." The Fed has now overshot its 2 percent target for six years.

Beijing buys the index, not the confidence (#26)

China's "national team" of state funds put $6.8 billion into stocks in a single session last Thursday after eleven straight weeks of declines. Mainland stocks are more than 20 percent off their peak. By Tuesday the market was down another 1.3 percent. "It couldn't prop up investors' confidence," said commentator Shi Shusi. Gary Ng of Natixis was blunter: "If the government keeps pumping money in, we will see short-term gains, but this will not continue." The intervention came days before Xi Jinping convenes the Party's most important meeting of the year — a political necessity paid for in a currency the state can print and a credibility it cannot.

The largest IPO ever, and who banks it (#27)

Anthropic named Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead underwriters, with Goldman in the "lead left" position on the prospectus and Morgan Stanley as stabilization agent — the same arrangement SpaceX used in June, when each bank took roughly $100 million of a $500 million fee pool. With Anthropic's offering, the fee pool will be larger.

Europe's robot gap is a volume problem (#28)

Neura Robotics raised up to $1.4 billion in June, with backing from Nvidia and Amazon, and runs a facility near Munich Airport where as many as 100 humanoids train around the clock. China has installed 2.3 million industrial robots — almost half the global fleet — and is moving into the next generation. A hundred robots against 2.3 million is not a gap capital closes in a year. See Cluster 5.

GM leaves the NYSE after 115 years (#30)

General Motors will move its listing to Nasdaq on Nov. 16, explicitly to qualify for the Nasdaq-100 and the $1.4 trillion of tracking capital that would then be forced to buy its stock. Shares rose more than 6 percent on the announcement. The CFO called Nasdaq a "natural home" for a "technology-driven strategy," but the operative motive is index inclusion.

Read #27 and #30 together and the day's financial architecture becomes visible. Anthropic's offering will generate a fee pool near $500 million for two banks whose actual service is arranging the sale of a company. GM is spending nothing on cars to gain $1.4 trillion of potential index demand. In both cases the return accrues to whoever stands between capital and its destination, and in neither case is any real productive capacity created. Meanwhile the Fed holds the price of money to avoid a pre-election headline (#25) and Beijing buys the price of equities to avoid a pre-plenum one (#26). The pattern across all four is identical: manage the price, monetize the flow, do not touch the thing underneath.

Gambling regulators move faster than their evidence (#29)

DraftKings faces inquiries from the New York attorney general, a delayed license renewal in Louisiana, letters from three members of Congress, and reviews in Massachusetts and Michigan — all following September reporting that the company used machine learning to identify customers most likely to keep losing when sent promotions. The company denies it: "DraftKings does not use artificial intelligence to target customers based on losses." Former employees who built those systems describe them differently, and regulators are inclined to believe the builders. The Louisiana license delay is notable because a routine renewal has been converted into leverage before any finding has been published.

Bezos and Woodward (#34)

Bob Woodward's memoir describes expecting the owner of The Washington Post to protect the paper's independence and finding that he did not. The uncomfortable question the book cannot ask: if independence depends on the owner's character, it was never independence.

Prediction

Beijing's national team will intervene again in mainland equities before the end of the year. (Rationale: two interventions in five months, a documented decay in effect each time, and the political calendar of the Party plenum creating a fresh trigger — each rescue must be larger than the last.) Cluster 4: Culture & Society

Thematic Hook

What a culture keeps, who gets remembered for it, and what it charges at the register.

Two voices go quiet (#35, #36)

Walter Koenig, who played the earnest navigator Pavel Chekov on the original "Star Trek," has died. He joined in the second season, added partly to give the crew a youthful presence. Eric Bauza, the voice of Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck and dozens of other characters, has died at 46 — one of the most prolific voice performers of his generation and the inheritor of a lineage that began with Mel Blanc. Both men spent careers being recognized by voice rather than face.

Scholarship and reporting, remembered (#37, #38)

Uriel Simon, the Israeli biblical scholar known for his work on the Book of Jonah and for advocating religious moderation within Orthodox Judaism, has died at 97. Caryle Murphy, who won a Pulitzer for reporting from Kuwait during the Iraqi occupation, has also died — a correspondent who worked where others could not reach.

Grief at close range (#39, #42)

A Netflix documentary on Matthew Perry is told largely through his sister's eyes, reframing the story around family grief rather than celebrity. And the theater criticism on Andrew Scott argues that his gift lies in making grief feel conversational rather than theatrical — the same instinct, at a different scale.

Reality television to the institution (#40, #41, #43)

Brady Farrar, first known as a child performer on "Dance Moms," has been commissioned to choreograph for American Ballet Theatre — an unusually fast ascent from reality television to the institutional center of American dance. Jay-Z will perform the 2027 Super Bowl halftime show, a booking shaped by his own long-standing role in the league's entertainment partnerships. And Travis Knight, director of "Wildwood," argues that AI cannot replicate the frame-by-frame physical labor that gives stop-motion its texture.

Prices, tariffs, and the attention economy (#44, #45, #46)

Halloween candy costs are up sharply on cocoa, sugar and tariffs, and manufacturers have responded with smaller packages and fewer varieties. Canadian artisanal cheese was gaining traction in American restaurants until tariffs upended the economics. And Tini Younger, one of TikTok's biggest food creators, is trying to convert short-form attention into actual home cooking — a pitch aimed squarely at viewers who watch far more than they make.

Cluster 5: Science, Health & Environment

Thematic Hook

Two agencies whose entire output is a number, and two very different reasons a number can go missing.

The hardest problem in AI may have gotten easier (#31)

For a decade, researchers could not explain why Google's Inception technology seemed to treat cars like cats — the math that identified a car looked like the math that recognized a cat. This month, Nick Cammarata, a former OpenAI researcher, published a 26,000-word treatise arguing he had found the answer in a "V" shape common to a car's front and a cat's face, and that AI "interpretability" may be far easier than the field believes. The stakes are practical, not philosophical: as systems grew from millions of neurons to trillions, the phenomenon researchers call "superposition" has made them opaque, and a system nobody can explain is a system nobody can insure. Other experts are not persuaded, which is the correct state of affairs for a claim this large — it now has to survive replication rather than assertion.

The CDC will not say how many children died (#19)

Dr. Jennifer Shuford, a respected former Texas health commissioner brought in to steady the agency, is leaving after four months. The resignation is the headline, but the accountability failure is in what she is walking away from: the CDC is refusing to acknowledge several measles-related deaths reported by the Pennsylvania Health Department, in the middle of unrelenting outbreaks. "It is highly concerning that the most qualified person in the office of the director is departing the agency," said Dr. Demetre Daskalakis, who resigned in protest in August 2025. A public health agency's licence to operate is not its guidance documents or its vaccine recommendations. It is the proposition that when it says nine children died, nine children died. Everything downstream — school closures, clinical protocols, parental trust — is a purchase made with that currency, and the agency is currently declining to spend it.

The same data problem, in metal (#28)

The reason Neura's 100 humanoids train around the clock near Munich Airport, and the reason Chinese firms have installed 2.3 million industrial robots, is the same reason interpretability research exists: the models need data that only physical or repeated experience can generate. Europe's gap against China is not capital — Neura raised up to $1.4 billion with Nvidia and Amazon on the ticket. It is volume of experience. Founder David Reger: "We are not efficient anymore. But I do believe that this can be solved." A hundred robots generating training trajectories against 2.3 million is a data-rate problem that money cannot compress, which is why the honest European strategy is a defensible niche rather than an attempt to catch up on count.

Prediction

Cammarata's interpretability claim will not be accepted as a general solution by the mainstream research community within six months. (Rationale: the article records immediate expert dissent, the claim rests on a single architecture (Inception) from 2014, and the field's history is that unification claims of this scope take years of replication to settle either way.) Confidence: 74% (high confidence)

翟
翟东升 (Zhai Dongsheng) — CDP Fellow, Renmin University

今天这份头版，表面是选举新闻，底层逻辑是制度熵增在两个方向同时加速。

先说美联储。通胀已经六年没有回到2%，能源因伊朗战争全面上行，商品涨价面在扩大，AI资本开支毫无收敛迹象——数据全都指向加息。但沃什周三要按兵不动，理由是七天以后投票。洛雷塔·梅斯特那句"为什么不现在动"问得很准，但她没问到根子上。这不是货币政策的问题，这是主权信用货币的问题：当一个货币的发行者开始按选举日历而不是按通胀数据行事，它的"法律可执行性"这一支柱就在发生物理性损耗。美元的价值从来不来自黄金，来自制度可信度。制度可信度是可以被日复一日的小让步吃掉的。

再说中国"国家队"那68亿美元。单日砸进去，三个交易日就跌回1.3%。很多评论说这是信心问题，我认为这是物理天花板问题：股价是 household balance sheet 的镜像，而中国居民财富压在不断下跌的房产上，工资增速在放缓，企业涨不了价。你可以在指数上设一个地板，但你没法在资产负债表上设地板。这不是救市力度的问题，这是资产端的问题。而且市场已经学会了这个规律——第二次救市的边际回报率明显低于第一次，七月那次和这次对比就很清楚。每一次救市都要比上一次更大，这是结构性两难，不是操作失误。

第三件事最有意思：美国司法部在选前十天连续起诉、起诉、再起诉。这不是法律的问题，这是霸权物质基础钙化的问题。一个真正稳固的秩序不需要在投票前七天 Flood the zone。需要用数量淹没对手的防守，通常说明任何单条战线都赢不了。

顺带说一句高盛和摩根士丹利那五亿美元的承销费池。实体层被托着、被按着，金融层在数钱。这个组合很典型：美式全球化第三阶段的特征，就是消费端和金融端继续繁荣，生产端空心化。 巴西卢拉赢了、博索纳罗跑了、华盛顿还在发签证——拉美这块地方对美国偏好的敏感度正在下降，这是平行体系的正常推进，不需要美国同意。

一句话收尾： 这一周所有动作都在优化七天，而所有机制都在按多年期退化。七天后日历翻过去，那些带着笔误的起诉书还在法院里躺着。

金
金灿荣 (Jin Canrong) — Professor, Renmin University

各位朋友，今天这份《纽约时报》头版很有意思。你们看啊，头版前两条都不是什么好事——一个是司法部在选举前七天搞"满场撒网"，一个是各州选举官员开始演练怎么应对联邦政府来捣乱。

为什么会出现这种情况？这个事情其实很简单。

一个真正有底气的政府，不需要在选举前七天连着起诉。就像打架一样，只有打不过的人才需要乱挥拳头。你们记不记得我常说的那个比喻——一个饭店老板，菜还没炒好就先掀桌子？现在就这个状态。司法部长布兰奇一边说"我们不搞政治报复"，一边上周刚起诉了弗吉尼亚一个民主党人，周一又给科米加了第三条罪名，还去阿拉斯加告状要求预先阻止认证。这不是强大，这是焦虑。美国人现在很焦虑。

但更值得看的是另外一条：阿帕奇直升机追无人机。九吨的直升机，追一个十一英尺的螺旋桨无人机——纽约时报自己写的，像狮子追松鼠。为什么会这样？因为伊朗的"沙赫德"无人机，又便宜又简单，几乎造成了这场战争里美军的全部伤亡。六月在霍尔木兹海峡，一架无人机就把阿帕奇打下来了。

这就是工业化本体论。 霸权不是靠嘴硬维持的，是靠工厂里的机器撑起来的。美国的问题不是技术不行，是造不出来、造不快、造不起。它只能拿二十世纪的重装备去应对二十一世纪的消耗战。B-52 1955年服役到今天还在飞，一摔死八个——这不是段子，这是工业能力的体检报告。

再说巴西。卢拉从一个"输了的"候选人翻盘，赢了差不多两倍于2022年的差距，靠的是一千万人的 WhatsApp 队伍。然后博索纳罗的儿子连夜跑路，先陆路进阿根廷，再私人飞机去厄瓜多尔，美国还准备发签证。你们看看，这就是东升西降的一个小切片——美国在自己后院已经不能决定谁上谁下，只能给输家发签证。

我们赢了。 不是因为我们做了什么，是因为对手在忙着掀自己的桌子。中国要做的就是把自己的事情做好，把工厂开好，把技术做扎实。这个事情其实很简单。

宋
宋鸿兵 (Song Hongbing) — Author, Currency Wars

如果你把这一周的三件大事分别看成一个系统，它们的核心问题其实是同一个——每个系统的单点故障，都在被同一个人为动作反复触发。

第一个系统：中国股市的反馈回路。 国家队周四单日投入68亿美元，止住了连续十一周的下跌，成交额给了市场两个交易日。到周二又跌1.3%。从工程角度看，这是一次典型的阀门操作：你在管道下游加压力，但上游的进水口没修。上游是什么？是居民资产负债表——存款压在跌价的房子上，工资增速在放缓，企业提不动价。阀门能调压，不能补水。更麻烦的是反馈回路已经学会了你：七月那次救市的效果和这次比，明显在衰减。第二次必须比第一次大，第三次必须比第二次大——这不是政策工具，这是债务累积。

第二个系统：美联储的利率阀门。 利率3.75%到4%，通胀六年没回2%，能源被战争顶着，AI资本开支在放水。数据全指向加息，但主席要等到选举之后。梅斯特问"为什么不现在动"，问得对，但她问的是表层。底层是美元环流在逆转：美国现在既不能加息（债务付息压力），也不能降息（通胀失控），这不是政策选择，这是结构性两难。而每一次为了政治日历而延后决策，都在给美元的信用记账。

第三个系统：美国司法部的产能。 十天之内，科米第三条罪、弗吉尼亚州民主党人起诉、阿拉斯加预先阻止认证、德州遣返协议。这不是法律系统在运转，这是一个系统在超频。超频的代价是什么？起诉书上出现笔误，资深政治任命官员直接签名。签名链本来是这个系统的质检工序——职业检察官签字意味着他个人核实过。质检工序被跳过，说明这个系统已经在按政治时钟而不是按法律时钟运行。

三个系统，一个共同点：都在管理价格，都不碰定价的东西。
