Cluster 1: Geopolitics & International Affairs

Thematic Hook

Washington is buying a domestic fuel price with foreign strategic assets, and every other capital on the board is repricing accordingly.

Trump's Push for Deals With Russia Powers His Frustration With Ukraine (#1)

David Sanger's reconstruction is the day's most consequential reporting: since roughly Oct. 9 the administration has shunted aside a bipartisan sanctions law signed weeks earlier, cut a deal to purchase Russian diesel, agreed on the vague outlines of a Lukoil transaction, and called for the replacement of Ukraine's president. Trump's own framing, delivered to reporters outside the White House, is that "Russia, frankly, has been fine on Ukraine" and "they haven't been doing much" — a statement made while Russian missiles and jet-powered drones target Kyiv's apartment blocks and its surviving grid. Sanger's key analytical move is to treat this not as inconsistency but as an intensifying desire: normalization with Moscow pursued as an end, with Ukraine as the obstacle to be moved.

Why Zelensky Took the Gloves Off This Time With Trump (#2)

Ukraine's answer is a strategic choice, not a mood. With strikes degrading its energy infrastructure, air defenses depleted, a hard winter coming, Pentagon-funded satellite internet restricted, intelligence-sharing gaps and delayed interceptors, Kyiv concluded that silence was being read as consent. A senior official's metaphor — "a last chance to sound an alarm as a tsunami approaches" — is exact: the value of the alarm is not that it stops the wave but that it establishes who saw it coming. American officials deny the disruptions; Sanger's and Varenikova's accounts cannot both be complete.

Trump's Betrayal of Ukraine (#36)

The editorial board supplies the chronology the news stories imply — the Oct. 9 diesel decision, Friday's executive order waiving other sanctions, and the Starshield service restrictions the White House denies ordering while Musk says only the military and ultimately the president could order. The board's judgment is that the motive is desperation over gas prices before the midterms. Read against Sanger, the board is describing a strategy it treats as a betrayal; Sanger is describing the same strategy as a preference. The facts are identical; only the intent attribution differs, and the intent question is what the next six weeks will settle.

It's Not Just France. Europe's Political Center Is Crumbling. (#4)

Steven Erlanger's frame is the continental version of the French story: centrist parties losing young people and salaried workers to populists of both flanks, amid low growth, high inflation, sizable debt and poor prospects for the young. His diagnosis is representational rather than purely economic — a center led by an educated elite that has lost its connection to the poor, the workers and the young, and governments that feel constrained in what they can do. That constraint is precisely what the French bond market converts from a sentiment into a price.

Taunting Trump Over Gas Prices, Iran Braces for Aftermath of U.S. Vote (#6)

Iran has found the cheapest available weapon against the United States: American retail fuel prices. State media, including the semiofficial Fars agency, is chronicling pump prices and quoting American polls on voter anger, and the Revolutionary Guards' open letter to Americans charges that Trump "keeps on telling Americans that the economy is booming and the prices will soon start coming down," adding: "This is, perhaps, his biggest lie." Tehran is not trying to win a military contest; it is campaigning in an American election, and the midterms are explicitly part of its calculus on both sides of the war.

In India, the Scion of a Political Dynasty Tries to Seize His Moment (#11)

Rahul Gandhi, 56, has spent years failing to be seen as more than the great-grandson of one prime minister and the grandson and son of two others. His current opening is a Gen Z street movement against what its leaders call a threat to the world's largest democracy — and his decision to join protesters and confront police is the first time his dynastic liability has converted into an asset. The strategic risk is symmetrical: a dynasty that leads a generational revolt either becomes its face or is discarded by it.

Trump Says He Asked Putin to Release an American Jailed in Russia (#12)

Trump says he has raised Stephen Hubbard — a Michigan teacher seized from his home in Ukraine and sentenced to more than six years as an alleged mercenary — directly with Putin, "at length," and asked for his release: "Let's see what happens." The function of this disclosure is diplomatic: it establishes that the Moscow channel produces humanitarian deliverables, which is the political cover any larger concession requires. It also confirms the channel is direct and personal, with no allied coordination visible.

Hegseth Makes Unannounced Visit to Israel for Talks With Netanyahu (#13)

The defense secretary met Netanyahu at Ovda air base a day after Trump publicly endorsed a prime minister in the middle of a tight campaign, with frustration in Washington mounting over a war in Iran that has produced neither a lasting deal nor a decision to escalate. Two electoral calendars are now coupled to one military posture, which is the structural problem: an American deployment is partly instrumental to an Israeli vote, and an Israeli vote is partly instrumental to an American one.

U.K. Sending Fighter Jets to Help Defend Saudi Cities From Houthi Attacks (#14)

Britain will deploy R.A.F. Typhoons for "defensive counter air missions" while explicitly ruling out offensive strikes against the Iran-backed Houthis. That is a commitment calibrated to signal alliance credibility to Riyadh without making Britain a belligerent — and a rules-of-engagement design that puts the restraint burden on the more expensive platform in a drone-and-missile environment.

Major Yemeni City Running Out of Food and Water as Houthis Encircle It (#15)

Taiz is in the third week of a siege, encircled by the Houthis, with residents reporting dwindling food, clean water and fuel and hospitals nearly out of fuel and medical supplies. A siege is a political weapon aimed at the claim that the Saudi-backed government can protect the people it governs. The strategic effect is achieved by waiting, which is precisely why it will not produce a decisive headline.

Voters in Oil-Rich Alberta Strongly Reject Leaving Canada (#9)

With 86 percent counted, 75.5 percent chose to remain in Canada rather than trigger a second vote on secession — a decisive result that strengthens Prime Minister Mark Carney's project of a unified country and removes the most credible North American separation threat from the board. It is the one unambiguous political outcome in the paper, and notably, no one bought it.

Prediction 1

The United States will not impose any new Russia sanctions before Nov. 3. (Rationale: three weeks of documented concessions — diesel sanctions relief Oct. 9, a Friday waiver, a Lukoil outline, pressure on Kyiv to stop refinery strikes — are all organized around a pre-election fuel price; a new sanction would reverse the only variable the strategy is trying to move. Trump's own framing that Russia "has been fine" leaves no domestic political basis for reimposition.) Confidence: 82% (high confidence)

Cluster 2: US Politics & Domestic Policy

Thematic Hook

Both parties are liquidating their balance sheets to argue about affordability, and the voters they are buying ads for have already stopped believing the aggregate numbers.

Across Farm Country, Economic Discontent Strains Republican Coalition (#7)

Lisa Lerer, Kevin Draper and Jess Bidgood report from Colorado, Missouri and Kansas, where tariffs, the war in Iran and the cost of tractors, fertilizer and fuel are fraying a decade-old bond: Trump won farmers and ranchers by double digits in all three presidential races, and Republican candidates are now defensive in places the party has not lost in a generation. The specific grievance — ranchers angered by the decision to drop import tariffs on foreign beef — is the tell. This is not ideological defection; it is a coalition being invoiced by the policy it supported.

Trump Announces $8 Billion Plan to Cover Farm Losses (#34)

At a Kansas rally, 15 days before the election, Trump announced $8 billion through a "Harvest Fuel Relief Program," funded via the Commodity Credit Corporation, reimbursing a "portion" of losses by a formula based on acres and crops planted. It is the second farmer bailout in two years, for damage caused by the administration's own policies. The formula is regressive by construction: it pays large row-crop operations proportionally more than the small farms whose discontent is the political emergency. As a signal it is loud; as policy it creates the expectation of the next check.

Trump Swings Through Deep-Red States Seeking to Reverse Lagging G.O.P. Enthusiasm (#21)

A six-day blitz through territory the party "did not view as competitive just a few months ago," with polling showing tariffs and the Iran war — and their effect on gas, diesel and grocery prices — gravely threatening the party's fortunes. Emily Davies and Jonathan Swan's detail is the strategic fact: the itinerary is a map of newly contested ground, and the president is campaigning where he should be playing defense, not offense.

Avalanche of G.O.P. Cash Keeps the Party Competitive Despite Its Problems (#18)

Theodore Schleifer and Matt Zdun frame the filings as a Rorschach test — Democrats leading in candidate-controlled money, which buys cheaper advertising and can be spent directly, and Republicans leading in super PAC money from groups tied to Musk, Trump and major donors, which buys frequency at higher rates. The Republican edge has let the party "club Democrats with advertising" despite the cost disadvantage. Net effect: the GOP is competitive on volume while losing on message, which is a survivable position only if turnout is a function of volume.

Aided by Supreme Court Ruling, R.N.C. Pumps $50 Million Into Midterms (#19)

The R.N.C. entered September with $126 million on hand and has been moving more than $44 million to the party's Hill committees between mid-August and mid-October, with total transfers above $50 million and many to be disclosed in Tuesday evening's filing. A recent Supreme Court decision enabled the structure. The Democratic National Committee is described as financially struggling and structurally incapable of the same move — a party-level asymmetry that will matter more in the final week than candidate-level totals.

Bloomberg Gives Democrats $80 Million in 11th-Hour Midterm Push (#20)

Michael Bloomberg is again the party's late-arriving white knight, directing about $80 million in September and October. The timing is the point: this money buys the closing week, when frequency matters more than persuasion. It also reveals the Democratic strategy — not a national message but insurance against being outspent in the last seven days.

Trump Hasn't Lowered Drug Prices for These Voters (#10)

Rebecca Robbins reports from Sanford and Oviedo, Florida, where James Evans, 37, a real estate agent without insurance coverage for most prescriptions and a registered Republican until last year, says the initiatives do not reach the daily ledger: "I don't see how this is going to affect most Americans on the daily." The mechanism of the political failure is precise: the administration's drug-price work is real but invisible at the pharmacy counter, and affordability politics is decided at the counter.

Justice Dept. Charges 28 in California With Voting-Related Crimes (#22)

Twenty-eight green card holders charged, 11 already arrested — roughly one defendant per million California residents. As evidence of the mass fraud the president describes, it is a rounding error; as an enforcement posture, it is a signal that the department intends to be visible in election administration. Devlin Barrett's framing — "part of an escalating effort to assert more control over the midterm elections" — is the operative sentence, because the deterrent effect on legal voters is the point of visibility.

Mahmoud Khalil Appeals to Supreme Court to Halt Deportation (#23)

The Columbia graduate arrested amid campus protests, a legal permanent resident married to an American, asks the justices to intervene — presenting another test of how far the administration can go in deporting people who are in the country legally. The case is now the leading legal vehicle for the entire campus-protest enforcement architecture, which is why its outcome will shape far more than one man's status.

U.S. Citizen Hospitalized After Being Detained by ICE, His Family Says (#24)

A California man his family says is a U.S. citizen was hospitalized in Long Beach after an altercation with ICE left him pinned to the ground in handcuffs, with local police called in as backup over vandalized vehicles and a person "in need of medical attention." It is the latest mistaken detainment as arrest numbers rise across the country, and the official account does not explain what produced the encounter.

Man Who Died in ICE Custody Was Denied Medicine, His Daughter Says (#25)

Emiliano Andrade-Covarrubias — heart condition, diabetes, high blood pressure — was found unconscious in his cell at the Rio Grande Processing Center in Laredo; his daughter says he had complained for months about the lack of medical care and told her, "They don't listen to me." Neither story is about immigration policy. Both are about whether a grievance channel exists.

U.N.C. Strikes Trump Deal to Keep Federal Funding (#28)

Nineteen federal inquiries closed, no admission of wrongdoing, no money paid, and commitments on admissions, DEI and the punishment of student groups — in exchange for preserved access to federal research funding. UNC becomes the latest school to align formally with the administration's vision for higher education, and the government said it would not use the agreement to control "academic speech or curricula." The operational weakness is that the commitments are obligations without stated boundary conditions, so the next administration can reopen all nineteen.

For Texas Schoolchildren, Minor Misbehavior Can Mean Criminal Charges (#8)

The Times/Express-News analysis of more than 47,000 referrals of children aged 10 to 13 since 2014 — about 4,800 in 2024-25, a third more than a decade earlier, most for threats, vape use or lashing out, few causing serious harm — is the domestic-policy failure with the longest tail. A one-third increase during a period of falling juvenile crime nationally is not a response to an emergency; it is a system substituting the justice docket for the principal's office.

Prediction 2

Republicans will retain control of the Senate. (Rationale: the party holds a structural advantage in the money that buys closing-week frequency — $50 million-plus in R.N.C. transfers plus a super PAC edge — while Democrats' advantage is concentrated in candidate-controlled funds that matter most in the House; Senate races are fewer, more nationalized, and more responsive to volume. This is a moderate-confidence call, not a high one: the polling leads Democrats hold in Texas, Ohio and Michigan have been narrow but consistent.) Confidence: 58% (moderate confidence)

Cluster 3: Business, Tech & Economy

Thematic Hook

The macro numbers are good, the household numbers are not, and every institution in between is discovering that the gap is where the politics lives.

3 Charts That Explain the Economy as Voters Head to the Polls (#33)

Ben Casselman's final pre-election snapshot is that the aggregates look strong — retail sales healthy in September, unemployment claims low, industrial output at a postpandemic high — which is precisely why it is grim for incumbents. "The economy is strong" and "voters are furious about prices" are not rival claims; they measure different objects. Retail sales record transactions, not the balance sheet behind them, and inelastic costs are being financed rather than avoided.

A 'Doom Loop' Threatens France as Populism Confronts Markets (#30)

In 2018 Macron answered the gilets jaunes with about $11 billion in tax cuts and wage increases. In 2026, after riots over underfunded schools turned violent, he has no such instrument, because investors are dumping French debt on doubts about the government's ability to manage its budget amid high rates and political uncertainty. The street is demanding the state spend and the bond market is demanding it not — the same event seen from opposite ends, and the constraint is now external.

Meta's Global Policy Chief to Leave After 15 Years (#31)

Joel Kaplan, 57, the former Bush adviser who built Meta's Washington operation and its relationship with the Trump administration, will leave next year after staying through the midterms. Zuckerberg's statement — "when he arrived, there was no playbook" — concedes that the playbook is the person. The operational risk is the assumption that a successor inherits a fifteen-year network rather than inheriting a desk.

Boeing's Large 737 Max Jet Secures U.S. Approval (#32)

The 737 Max 10 is certified after a six-year delay, clearing Boeing to compete with an Airbus that used the interval to become the world's largest manufacturer. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy's statement cites "extensive safety reviews — including thousands of hours of test flights." The six years are the point: after the Max 8 crashes, the rebuilt certification process was designed to be slow, and an industry that treats that slowness as a cost will keep paying for it in casualties and groundings.

Trump Tariffs on Canada Spark Latest Lawsuit Over Trade Powers (#35)

The Liberty Justice Center's fourth major tariff challenge targets the Canada duties under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — a provision dormant since the 1930s that Trump became the first president to invoke, in September. The attack is on the statutory basis rather than the tariff level, which is the more dangerous kind: a rate can be negotiated, a legal authority can be removed.

The Cover-Up: How a Trillion-Dollar Industry Launders Illegal Gold (#3)

Justin Scheck and Lynsey Addario's investigation, with photography from illegal mines in Madagascar, shows gold banned from the legitimate market flowing to market anyway — through an industry fiction that Wall Street and governments profit from, dug in part by thousands of children in violation of international law. The companion piece's title is the finding: "To Find Criminals and Child Exploitation in Gold Mines, We Just Had to Look." The verification step in the global gold chain does not exist, which means the fix is a site visit, not an ethics policy.

Prediction 3

Boeing will not deliver its first 737 Max 10 to a customer before April 2027. (Rationale: certification is the gate that just opened, not the end of the chain; a six-year gap implies a production and supplier ramp that has not been running, and airlines typically need months between certification and first delivery for training and configuration.) Confidence: 72% (high confidence)

Cluster 4: Culture & Society

Thematic Hook

The institutions that used to absorb shock between the powerful and the vulnerable are gone, and the two ends are now meeting directly.

France Extends Curfews in Major Cities After a Night of Calm (#16)

Paris, Marseille and Lille extended curfews after they helped quell rioting, with Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu telling lawmakers, "These localized curfews are efficient," and vowing a "massive" security deployment for Saturday's planned demonstrations. The measure is being judged by whether last night was calm, not by whether the schools that started the protests are funded — which is why the calm is a pause rather than a settlement.

Student Protesters in France Have a Strange New Slogan. What Does It Mean? (#17)

"Bismillah, c'est les ténèbres" — "In the name of God, it's the darkness" — appears on cardboard signs across France, a mash-up that Zane Irwin reads as a generational shibboleth rather than a program. A movement whose unifying text is deliberately untranslatable cannot negotiate, which is a fact about its composition: it is a cohort expressing a rupture, not a constituency presenting demands.

France Comes Unglued, Again (#38)

Bret Stephens runs the American-liberal inventory of the French model — universal health care, cheap day care, affordable universities, strong worker protections, high taxes, green investment, constrained defense — and concludes the model is no longer plausible, with presidential elections potentially pitting Mélenchon against Le Pen. His is the mirror image of Erlanger's structural account: where Erlanger describes a representational failure, Stephens describes a product failure.

Woman Who Says She Was Raped at Cornell Sends Message to Faculty Senate (#29)

Jane Doe's two-page letter says she felt "unsupported" and "disbelieved" by President Michael Kotlikoff, and recounts that his initial response, on being told of the alleged assault at a fraternity house, was "along the lines of, 'Why did she wait so long to report it?'" The letter arrives as the faculty senate considers a rare no-confidence vote — meaning the institution's judgment will turn not on the lawsuit but on the quality of its first contact with the complainant.

I Have Never Seen the Online Right So Happy (#37)

Nathan Taylor Pemberton identifies the mechanism precisely: unable to dispute the underlying narrative, including a lurid fraternity group chat, the online right has set out to disqualify the witness rather than rebut the claim — "hoax," "witch hunt," and, in Alex Jones's coinage, "super slut." The essay's insight is that this requires no evidence because its currency is not evidence. It is a standing operation conducted by people who have found, in his phrase, "a woman to hate on."

A Matter of Values, at Cornell and Beyond (#40)

A letter to the editor asks the question the coverage cannot answer: what does it take for a roomful of educated young men to recognize that a woman could be in danger? Not a Snapchat message advertising access to her body, and not a group chat joking about it first. The disturbing detail is the banter — the casualness before the fact, which is where an institution's culture actually lives.

They Budgeted for Rent, Not All the Extra Fees (#39)

Tracie McMillan's San Francisco renter, Pauline Lee, a half-century resident, on charges appearing on her rent ledger: "I never had no problem with bills until I moved here." The context is the number that should be in every affordability story — rents up 36 percent since the start of the pandemic, before add-on fees migrated from utilities to once-included services. The junk fee is a mechanism for raising the price without raising the rent, which is to say: for keeping the number that gets measured unchanged.

Prediction 4

Cornell's faculty senate will hold or formally schedule a no-confidence vote in President Kotlikoff before Nov. 15. (Rationale: the letter was written specifically to inform that vote, the senate is already considering it, and the underlying lawsuit plus a two-page complainant letter create schedule pressure that a deliberative body rarely resists for a month.) Confidence: 64% (moderate confidence)

Cluster 5: Science, Health & Environment

Thematic Hook

Money and capability are arriving faster than the capacity to verify either — one claim has a paper trail, one does not, and the third is a check with no system behind it.

Start-Up Claims to Use A.I.-Created Viruses to Treat a Patient (#5)

For the first time, researchers say viruses designed by artificial intelligence have been administered to a human — a phage given to a patient with a deadly bacterial infection, announced by the start-up Radical Numerics in a technical report on its own website, with no information about the patient or the treating physicians, citing confidentiality. Phages attack bacteria, and the hope is that this one kills the infection. The claim is plausible and extraordinary in the same breath, and the appropriate posture is neither dismissal nor celebration: it is a list of what would settle it — outcome, institutional review, independent sequencing of the administered virus.

A.I. Fortunes Could Bring Critical Funds to Global Health. The Scramble Is On. (#26)

Stephanie Nolen reports a gold rush conducted in private dining rooms at the U.N. General Assembly: donors asking veteran health leaders what they would do with another billion dollars, or $5 billion, and which projects are "shovel ready" to absorb $100 million or more. With governments led by the United States sharply reducing support, the money is real and the absorptive capacity is not — a senior executive described the scramble as "a gold rush." The likeliest failure mode is not fraud; it is speed applied to delivery systems built for a fraction of the money now being discussed.

Kennedy's Vaccine Plan Could Cause Surge in Hepatitis B, Study Finds (#27)

Ending universal newborn hepatitis B immunization, as Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has recommended, would sharply increase chronic infections and put more than half of Americans at risk of the disease by age 20, according to a study published Monday. The existing protocol is a public-health achievement of unusual precision — first dose within 24 hours of birth, within 12 hours for infants of infected mothers — and last December a Kennedy-chosen advisory panel recommended parental discretion, "no earlier than 2 months of age," for infants of hepatitis-B-negative mothers. The dispute is formally empirical; structurally it is about which body's judgment confers standing.

Prediction 5

No independent confirmation of the AI-designed phage claim — peer-reviewed publication, regulatory disclosure, or an identified treating institution — will appear before Jan. 1, 2027. (Rationale: the company has already declined to identify the patient or physicians on confidentiality grounds, which is the same posture that blocks independent verification; the base rate for a single-patient n-of-1 company announcement reaching peer review within ten weeks is very low, and the intervening period includes the year-end publishing gap.) Confidence: 86% (near-certain)

翟东升 (Zhai Dongsheng) — CDP Fellow, Renmin University

一、特朗普的"俄罗斯转向"不是外交问题，是主权信用问题

底层逻辑是：美国正在用战略信用去换加油站的油价。取消俄柴油制裁、签豁免令、谈卢克石油、压乌克兰停止打击炼油厂——这一连串动作的支付方不是俄罗斯，也不是乌克兰，而是美国自己那套制裁体系的法律可执行性（legal enforceability）。制裁之所以是武器，是因为所有人相信它明天还在。一旦总统可以为一场中期选举把它拆掉，这个武器就贬值了。

这不是乌克兰的问题，这是美元体系的问题。美元的价值来自美国制度信用——法律的确定性、政策的连续性、盟友的可预期性。这三根柱子，今天在头版上同时被削薄：制裁可以随时豁免（法律确定性受损）、盟友可以被单方面抛下（可预期性受损）、总统公开说"俄罗斯其实一直很克制"（事实基础受损）。

二、法国的债券市场，是全球主权信用重定价的第一个样本

法国2018年能拿110亿美元买平静，2026年拿不出来——因为投资者在抛法债。这说明主权信用的定价权已经从议会转移到了债券市场，而债券市场不讲政治，只讲偿付能力。这是物理天花板：再高明的政治技巧也穿不过利率约束。

三、结论

美国当前的做法是典型的边际回报率归零：用越来越大的战略资产，换越来越小的国内政治收益。八亿美元的农业补贴、五千多万美元的党产转移、八十亿美元的布隆伯格支票——钱在加速，效果在衰减。这是制度熵增的典型症状。中方的正确姿势仍然是"把自己的事情做好"：当对手开始消耗自己的信用存量时，你不需要出手，只需要不犯错。

金灿荣 (Jin Canrong) — Professor, Renmin University

一、今天的头版很有意思

各位朋友，你们看今天的《纽约时报》头版，有两件事摆在一起：一件是特朗普在拆自己的对俄制裁，一件是他跑到堪萨斯发八十亿美元的农业补贴。这两件事放在一起看，这个事情其实很简单——他在花钱买选票，而且是用别人的钱、别人的安全、别人的信用去买。

为什么？因为十五天以后要投票了，而美国老百姓最关心的不是乌克兰，是加油站的牌价。

二、一个类比

打个比方：一个开饭馆的老板，为了让客人觉得菜价便宜，先把自家后厨的灶台拆了当柴烧。今天这顿饭是便宜了，明天这馆子就开不下去了。

特朗普现在干的就是这个事。欧洲的盟友看着，日本人看着，沙特人看着，全世界都看明白了一件事：美国的承诺是有保质期的，保质期到选举日为止。

三、欧洲那边更热闹

法国在宵禁，债券市场在抛法债，学生喊的口号连自己都解释不清——"以真主之名，这是黑暗"。这是什么？这是整个中间层没了。老百姓和政府之间原来有学校、有工会、有政党，现在这些都没了，两头直接撞上。

百年大变局讲的正是这个：不是中国要颠覆什么，是西方自己那套中介机制在瓦解。

四、结论

美国现在最焦虑的地方在哪儿？不是中国，是它自己那套体系转不动了。军工造不出新飞机，波音737MAX一个型号审了六年；党争要靠八十亿美元的支票来维持；盟友关系要靠撤制裁来换油价。

东升西降不是口号，是每天的头版。中国要做的还是那句话——把自己的事情做好。我们赢了，不是赢在嘴上，是赢在工厂里的机器还在转。

宋鸿兵 (Song Hongbing) — Author, Currency Wars
