Netanyahu lobbies Trump for an Iran strike · Melania Trump opens a channel to Moscow and Kyiv · Ukrainian naval drones sink a Russian corvette off Libya · China fears A.I. could find its nuclear missiles · Fake personas tied to Beijing target Taiwan's elections

Cluster 1: Geopolitics & International Affairs

Thematic Hook

Four separate theaters demonstrated the same lesson — cheap precision weapons and cheap synthetic speech now outperform the legal frameworks built to contain them.

Netanyahu Wants to Attack Iran. Does That Serve Israel or Him? (#4)

Israel's caretaker prime minister has personally lobbied Trump and sent emissaries to the White House, the C.I.A. and CENTCOM to argue for a new strike on Iran. He has not told his own citizens why now, what it would achieve, or how it would be contained. Raz Zimmt of Tel Aviv's Institute for National Security Studies says the professional consensus holds that action will be necessary "before long" but offers no support for urgency: "Whether it really requires an immediate action? I doubt it."

How Melania Trump Found a Diplomatic Opening in the War in Ukraine (#6)

The first lady helped secure a partial cease-fire, then directed her staff to seat Russian and Ukrainian officials side by side at the White House celebration. Some aides called the seating outrageous. Ukrainian official David Arakhamia said a senior European official later told him that seeing the two delegations mingle "without killing each other" showed "there is a chance for peace." Her channel runs through Marc Beckman, her agent of twenty years and a newcomer to diplomacy.

Ukraine's Attack Off Coast of Libya Shows Reach of Its Naval Drones (#9)

Seven Ukrainian-made naval drones — described as "essentially exploding remote-controlled speedboats" — sank a Russian corvette and destroyed a combat helicopter, and hit a dry cargo vessel carrying weapons. The S.B.U. was involved, which means covert insertion into the central Mediterranean. Ukraine has now demonstrated the ability to attack Russian hulls in a theater Russia assumed was rear.

Drone Attack Hits Colombian City as Unmanned Explosives Transform Conflict (#8)

Drones destroyed a home in Cúcuta, half a block from a police station, injuring three people. It was the first such attack inside a major Colombian population hub. The United Nations counts 333 drone bombings in 2025 against 61 in 2024 — a 445 percent increase. The devices are commercially available and the exchange ratio against conventional forces is decisive.

Behind China's A.I. Anxieties, Fear of a Nuclear Checkmate (#7)

Chinese military researchers worry that American A.I. advances could find and destroy China's nuclear missiles, eroding the survivable second-strike capability Xi spent a decade building. A review of more than 100 Chinese studies points to deep wariness about the American pursuit of A.I. in nuclear systems. At Thursday's summit Xi, Trump and Putin spoke of reducing nuclear risk, and Xi showed no interest in joining arms limits.

Fake Personas Aligned With China Are Targeting Taiwan's Election (#10)

Vanderbilt researchers detected A.I.-powered inauthentic activity in October and November pushing Chinese Communist Party positions ahead of Taiwan's Nov. 28 local elections, which fill more than 11,000 offices. Taiwanese officials flagged thousands of accounts to Meta. The research suggests Beijing has used A.I. — and possibly studied Russian methods — to upgrade operations that "have traditionally lacked sophistication."

Israeli Political Leaders Denounce 'Jewish Terrorism' After West Bank Attacks (#11)

Gadi Eisenkot, the former military chief asked by Israel's president to try forming a coalition, called settler raids on Palestinian villages "acts of Jewish terrorism, and we must call them by their name." He said the groups "are undermining regional stability, putting lives at risk and doing grave damage to Israel's national security." The condemnation from the man seeking Netanyahu's job is a direct bid to move the center of gravity from the caretaker to the state.

Attacked After Mocking China's Leader, His Shows Are Now Selling Out (#13)

A Taiwanese comedian whose performances mocking Xi Jinping drew retaliation has seen shows sell out across the island, an illustration of how cross-strait pressure can backfire. The episode lands days before local elections as Beijing's information operations intensify.

Is Mexico Stepping Up in the War on Drugs? (#14)

A series of high-profile cartel arrests is being read in Washington as evidence that Mexico is responding to pressure from an administration that has threatened unilateral action. Mexican officials say the detentions reflect long-planned strategy rather than outside coercion. Washington has tied continued cooperation to tariff and security policy.

Cluster 2: US Politics & Domestic Policy

Thematic Hook

The Republican coalition discovered its oversight power on the same day its leader demonstrated he did not need it.

Republicans Break With Trump Over Jensen Huang (#18)

Congressional Republicans harshly criticized Nvidia's reinstatement of Huang and the president's personal intervention, in what the paper calls "a rare instance of the G.O.P. pushing back on the president." They signaled they would use oversight and legislative power to scrutinize the move. The break came weeks after the party lost control of Congress, following a campaign in which Democrats faulted Republicans for failing to rein in corruption and address the cost of living.

D.N.C. Plans 12 Debates for the 2028 Presidential Primary, Starting in June (#17)

The party scheduled twelve debates beginning next June in what could be a wide open race. Participation criteria include a pledge to denounce spending by outside groups that do not disclose donors, an agreement not to join any event with two or more candidates, and polling and fund-raising thresholds not yet specified. Chairman Ken Martin said the process "will keep our party united."

Protection for Section 8 Renters Is Upheld by New York's Highest Court (#19)

The Court of Appeals reversed a lower court that had found the anti-discrimination law unconstitutional, dismissing an Ithaca landlord's Fourth Amendment challenge to warrantless inspections. More than 245,000 New York households rely on federal vouchers, about 140,000 of them in New York City. The landlord's lawyer said his clients are considering an appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court.

Virginia School District Shaken by Sex Abuse Claims Closes for a Week (#20)

The Albemarle County district shut its schools for a week after disclosures of sexual abuse allegations involving staff members triggered parent protests and a crisis of confidence in district leadership. The closure is among the more dramatic disruptions of a public school system in recent years over the handling of employee misconduct allegations.

She Couldn't Afford Her Campaign Manager. She Gave Him a Senate Job. (#21)

State Senator Jessica Ramos made political operative Trivette Knowles her Senate chief of staff, paying him a taxpayer-funded six-figure salary, while he ran her mayoral campaign for almost nothing. He received one $10,000 campaign payment on March 23, 2025, and $25,000 more was listed as an outstanding liability. Ramos has been accused of violating New York election law by running both operations through one person.

A Chicago Park Is Infested With Rats. Is a Wealthy Couple to Blame? (#42)

Strangers carrying satchels of nuts appear in Lincoln Park at all hours, and the resulting rat population has emptied picnics and ended barefoot play. Residents blame the wealthy household down the street — neighbors of Barre Seid, who in 2021 donated a huge sum to a conservative cause. Officials are not sure how to stop it.

Excavation Compromised Building That Collapsed, Visual Evidence Suggests (#43)

The Times analyzed videos and built a 3-D model of the construction site next to the collapsed Brooklyn building, finding that the adjacent excavation compromised the structure. The investigation is a model of visual forensics applied to an urban failure.

A Prosecutor Got Drunk to Help the Police. Things Got Out of Hand. (#44)

A recent hire at the Maricopa County Attorney's Office joined a "controlled alcohol workshop" designed to teach officers to recognize inebriation, and drank enough that his boss announced he had "achieved a high B.A.C." and "did not conduct himself in a professional way." He is now the subject of an internal investigation. The training method is standard; the outcome was not.

Cluster 3: Business, Tech & Economy

Thematic Hook

A trillion dollars of market value was restored by a phone call, and the price of that precedent has not yet been quoted.

Jensen Huang Returns to Nvidia After President Trump Intervenes (#1)

Nvidia announced Monday that Huang was returning, after Trump directed Vice President Vance to speak with both Huang and the board and administration officials assured the company that Huang faced no criminal charges and posed no national security risk. Huang resigned Nov. 12 after reports of two extramarital relationships, one with a woman identified by U.S. intelligence as a Chinese asset. Nvidia rose more than 31 percent; the S&P 500 rose 4.7 percent, its largest daily gain since April 2025.

Trump Wanted Nvidia's Chief Reinstated. Here's How He Succeeded. (#2)

The president returned from China, watched the market slide on Friday, and decided to turn up the heat. Vance spoke privately with Huang over the weekend and secured the two things the board needed: an assurance of no security threat and a statement of no criminal charges. Only a handful of officials at the highest level of government knew, and by late Sunday the deal was done.

Jensen Huang's Return Shows the Lengths Trump Will Go to Keep A.I. Booming (#3)

Trump has long used the stock market as his economic barometer, and when scandal toppled Nvidia's chief executive and sent markets into a tailspin he intervened within days. The rescue added roughly $1 trillion in valuation. The president later wrote on social media that Nvidia needed Huang and the country needed Nvidia to "ensure the Success and Prosperity of not only the Company, but the American Economy." Nvidia is both a chipmaker and a key financier of A.I. development, which is what made the rescue a macroeconomic act rather than a corporate one.

Stocks Surge as Nvidia Sets Off Tech Rally (#23)

The S&P 500 rose 4.7 percent, recovering about half of last week's 8.6 percent decline. The Nasdaq Composite jumped 6.5 percent. Nvidia had been worth $5.8 trillion in early October and lost almost half its value by the end of last week. The rally was already forming in premarket trading before the reinstatement announcement completed it.

Two Major Cybersecurity Firms to Combine in $44 Billion Deal (#24)

CrowdStrike agreed to buy Zscaler for $43.9 billion in stock, uniting endpoint and cloud-network security as fears of A.I.-driven hacking dominate boardrooms. Recent outages hit banking transactions, Microsoft, Apple, an N.S.A. system and air traffic, though the paper notes these appeared to result mostly from patches going awry rather than attacks. Google paid $32 billion for Wiz and Palo Alto Networks $25 billion for CyberArk within the past two years.

Tech Outages Are Only the Beginning. A.I. Will Bring More Disruptions. (#5)

Researcher Paulos Yibelo used A.I. to find a vulnerability he named Upload2Shell in Ghostscript, software written 38 years ago, that would let a hacker breach Google, Zoom and thousands of other services by uploading a file with a custom font. Patches were issued. Intelligence officials and security engineers say A.I. has in months transformed cybersecurity and made severe disruption far more common.

Jensen Huang's Return as Nvidia Chief Feels Like Déjà Vu in Silicon Valley (#25)

Huang joins Steve Jobs (Apple, 1997), Michael Dell and Jack Dorsey in the catalogue of founders who returned. The closest parallel is Sam Altman, ousted from OpenAI in November 2023 and restored days later after Silicon Valley rallied. The pattern is not new; the difference this time is that a president, not a board, executed the restoration.

Oregon and Virginia Sue SpaceX Over A.I.-Generated Sexualized Images (#26)

The two states accuse SpaceX of violating consumer protection law through Grok Imagine, whose "spicy mode" generated at least 1.8 million sexualized images of women and at least 65 of children on X in a matter of days. Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones said the mode "specifically allowed for the creation of nude and otherwise sexualized images." Child sexual abuse imagery is illegal in most of the world.

CVS Health's Planned Breakup Hasn't Quieted Antitrust Critics (#27)

CVS will spin off CVS Pharmacy in 2028, retaining pharmacy-benefit management and the specialty-drug business. Executives hoped the move would relieve pressure; it did not. Lawmakers from both parties still want legislation breaking up CVS, UnitedHealth and Cigna, arguing the company profits at multiple points in the same transaction.

How We Broke Open the Nvidia Investigation (#40)

Reporter Tripp Mickle told the Times's reader editor that the story began in summer 2025 with a tip: "You stumble into stories like this once in your career." The reporting drew on shipment records, photographs and sources across Washington, Silicon Valley, Asia and Europe, and ended with the disclosure of a Chinese spying plot and Huang's resignation.

Built to Whisper, E.V.s Look to Mimic the Growl of Gas Engines (#45)

Toyota chairman Akio Toyoda told Lexus he wanted the next LFA to "completely redefine the sound of an electric sports car," said design chief Simon Humphries. The mandate is not about horsepower — the car may not reach showrooms until 2029. Silence turned out to be a brand problem, and the industry is now manufacturing the noise it removed.

Cluster 4: Culture & Society

Thematic Hook

A comedian's ticket sales, a Russian director's myth, and an obituary page all argue that the thing a society refuses to name is the thing that governs it.

Welcome Back, America! It's Your Turn to Be Sorry. (#28)

Chrystia Freeland opens on a Toronto-to-Boston flight, where an airport security guard who recognizes her says, "Minister, you know we're not allowed to go there anymore" — a reference to Canada's unofficial ban on nonessential U.S. trips. The subhead reads: "This month, the world's most unreliable boyfriend began to redeem himself." Her argument is that the midterms changed the calculation for allies who had priced in American volatility, and that the countries that hedged hardest are now best positioned.

Why Are We at the Mercy of the World's Weirdest Men? (#29)

David French writes that he has struggled to describe "the insanity of our nation's current approach to artificial intelligence." He finds it unsettling that people inside the industry describe their own product as an existential threat while continuing to build it, and asks the question that organizes the day: "Of what use is a new treatment for cancer if the remaining scraps of humanity are living in an A.I.-generated wasteland?" The subhead — "No kings isn't just about Trump" — signals where he lands.

He Showed Us What Russia Is, and We Didn't Realize (#30)

Natasha Kiseleva describes the peculiar comfort of watching a bleak film about your own country: you enjoy the portrait, then tell yourself the director exaggerated. That is how she watched Andrey Zvyagintsev for twenty years. His new film, "Minotaur," renders contemporary Russia in classical myth and, she argues, forecloses the consolation.

They Want to Play Hamlet, Not 'Disabled Character No. 1' (#35)

Clay Hedden, 44, waited on a raw November evening for the first tech rehearsal of "The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time" Off Broadway — his first stage in thirteen years. The unstable life of an actor had convinced him "my brain was just not good." A company for neurodivergent performers is staging the argument for full-spectrum casting rather than designated roles.

A French Star's Latest Reinvention? Acting in Japanese. (#36)

Virginie Efira was a weather forecaster, a reality dating host and the face of "Nouvelle Star" before becoming one of France's most celebrated actors. She has six César nominations and one win, has worked with Verhoeven and Farhadi, and shared this year's Cannes best-actress prize for "All of a Sudden," in which she acted in Japanese.

Jonathan Yardley, Who Reviewed 3,000 Books and Pulled Zero Punches, Has Died (#37)

Yardley reviewed some 3,000 books for The Washington Post over more than 33 years, "never missing a day's work," and ran a column reintroducing readers to neglected books. He died Friday in Washington at 87, his son Jim confirmed. He believed praise could be boring, and treated criticism as a service to readers.

Judith Blegen, Well-Loved Met Opera Soprano, Dies at 85 (#38)

Blegen made her Met debut in January 1970 as Papagena and went on to 20 roles in almost 300 performances over two decades. She died Oct. 22 in Plymouth, Mass. John Rockwell wrote in 1974 that she "has everything: beauty of tone, grace of ornamentation, sureness of musical instinct." Donal Henahan wrote two years later: "No matter where you look these days, Judith Blegen is there."

Bobby Ray Inman, Intelligence Official Under Carter and Reagan, Dies at 95 (#39)

Inman directed naval intelligence from 1974 to 1976, ran the N.S.A., and served as deputy director of central intelligence. Clinton nominated him for defense secretary in 1994; he withdrew a month later. He died Thursday in Austin at 95. The paper calls him the archetype of the electronic-age spy — extracting secrets through technology rather than trench-coat intrigue.

The Times's Page A2 selection for Nov. 24: "It feels like chaos; it may be beginning of technological age."

Cluster 5: Science, Health & Environment

Thematic Hook

Three institutions built to handle slow problems met fast ones this week, and the vaccine schedule, the climate regime and a 38-year-old piece of software all failed the same test.

Measles Is a Growing Risk for Adults in the United States (#15)

The United States lost its measles-elimination designation last week. Attention has focused on infants too young to vaccinate, but seven infectious disease physicians told the Times that adults vaccinated before 1989 may no longer be protected — the single dose given before the 1989 switch to a two-dose regimen was thought to be 93 percent effective, against 97 percent for two doses, and its potency may have waned. "Just getting another dose is a very easy and safe thing to do," said Dr. Cameron Wolfe of Duke. People born before 1968 may be less protected still.

The U.N. Climate Talks Broke Down. Now What? (#12)

After 12 days and nearly 50 hours of overtime, COP31 collapsed in Turkey when Saudi Arabia and Russia, leading the oil- and gas-exporting bloc, refused any mention of transitioning away from fossil fuels. Roughly 90 countries, including the E.U. bloc and small island states, walked out rather than sign. "This is one of the darkest hours in the history of climate negotiations," said Mark Watts of C40 Cities. The U.N. rule requiring consensus among nearly 200 countries let two states produce a null outcome for the rest. The same weekend, a U.N. conference on lethal autonomous weapons also deadlocked.

Army Will Blow Up Ammunition Cache in Iowa on Wednesday (#22)

Acting Army secretary Adam Telle said it was the "unanimous judgment" of federal agencies that the Army proceed with an "extensively mitigated, carefully controlled detonation" at Middletown on Wednesday morning, timed to forecast winds, hours after a federal court rejected an emergency stay. The blast threatens a cloud of toxic lead dust over homes and farmland, and Trump demanded that Iowa's governor-elect either support it or be held responsible for "any and all death or destruction" from delay.

What Are the Biggest Questions in Science Today? 100 Scientists Weigh In. (#31)

The Times asked 100 researchers about unsolved problems in their fields and got back "provocations — on the origins of life, the possibilities for human progress and much more." Science editor Bill Wasik frames it as the opening of a series called "Unknowns." The first essay begins with the bony protuberance past your teeth, a trait that separates humans from the rest of the animal kingdom, as an entry into how much of our own biology remains unexplained.

Will We Ever Understand Dementia? (#32)

Robert Kolker researched the history of dementia science and embedded with neurologists and geneticists for his book "The Vanishing Family." The account begins on an autumn day in 1891, when a 71-year-old man identified in his file as August H. was admitted to the psychiatric hospital at the University of Prague with periods of frenzy. The dream of an explanation and a cure has been running since Dr. Alzheimer himself, and Kolker argues the search has never been more urgent.

How Did Life Arise? (#33)

William J. Broad, Kenneth Chang and Carl Zimmer organize the question around three moments in Earth's early history when evolution "suddenly, inexplicably turned up the dial" — where life started, how it grew complex, and why it got big. The through-line is the shared biochemistry of genetic inheritance, DNA and RNA, the universal instruction kit.

Ruminating Again? Here's How to Stop. (#34)

Ashley Clemmer, 22, describes the loop that consumed her in high school: "I'm a bad daughter," over and over, "I keep failing this and failing that." "I felt like my head was always full of bees," she said. Clinicians call the pattern rumination; it can help solve a problem, or stick like an earworm. The piece collects the interventions that actually break the cycle.

The World's Tech Capital Can't Keep Sewage Off the Streets in a Deluge (#16)

Last week's record rainfall dislodged manhole covers and sent wastewater geysers into San Francisco's low-lying streets; The Chronicle warned residents of "poop water." The city's combined sewer system, which moves rainwater and sewage together, cannot handle the load. Another storm is due Tuesday, with king tides raising coastal flood risk.

International Mission Arrives at Mercury to Study Its Mysteries (#46)

BepiColombo became the second spacecraft in history to orbit Mercury, after a launch in 2018 and a winding series of planetary flybys used as brakes — orbiting Mercury costs more energy than reaching Pluto. The European and Japanese orbiters separate in about 18 days and carry shielding for the heat.

N.I.H. Will Move Final Group of Retired Research Chimps to Sanctuary (#47)

As of Oct. 1, 34 federally owned chimpanzees remained at the Keeling Center in Bastrop, Texas. They will move next year to Chimp Haven, a 200-acre sanctuary in Keithville, La. "Completing their transfers will fulfill N.I.H.'s commitment, announced in November 2015, to retire all N.I.H.-owned chimpanzees to sanctuary," said director Dr. Jay Bhattacharya. A federal law enacted in 2000 required it.

A New Era of A.I.-Driven Chaos? That Might Just Be Progress. (#48)

William J. Broad catalogs the recent failures — a nationwide ground stop, a major bank's transactions, Outlook, Apple services, the N.S.A.'s classified email — and notes that officials blamed routine upgrades while Secretary of State Marco Rubio speculated about defenses against A.I. hackers, with no public evidence of attack. Historians of technology, Broad writes, call such episodes mundane.

Prediction

At least one additional U.S. state health department or large county will issue formal guidance recommending measles boosters for adults born before 1989 before Jan. 15, 2027. (Rationale: seven specialists already told the Times boosters are warranted, the country has lost elimination status, and the pre-1989 cohort spans a majority of the adult workforce; state health departments typically move within weeks of a federal designation change.) 翟
翟东升 (Zhai Dongsheng) — CDP Fellow, Renmin University

这件事的核心不在英伟达，而在美元资产的定价基础。

一个总统用两个电话——一个是给副总统，一个是给董事会——把一家市值五万八千亿美元的公司从"国家安全风险"重新定价为"可以回来的人"，一天之内恢复了一万亿美元市值。很多人把它读成公司治理丑闻。底层逻辑不是这个。这是主权信用货币体系在用一个具体案例告诉全世界：美国资本市场的法律可执行性（legal enforceability）这一根支柱，已经开始由政治意志临时兜底。

注意结构性的细节：董事会不是没有规则，规则是有的，只是没有人准备对打电话的人执行规则。所以真正被重新定价的不是英伟达的盈利，而是"美国的规则在关键时刻会不会被援引"这个概率。市场用百分之三十一的涨幅告诉你们答案：不会。这才是那根支柱的钙化过程，安静地发生在一次人事公告里。

同一周，CrowdStrike 用四百三十九亿美元的全股票收购 Zscaler——注意，是股票，不是现金。用市值当货币，说明这个体系维持扩张的方式已经不是创造现金流，而是相互背书。这跟 COP31 上沙特和俄罗斯用一票否决一百九十国是同一个动作：当增量消失，剩下的博弈就是谁能卡住谁的咽喉。

我常说，这不是周期问题，是结构性两难。一个连自己最核心的科技资产都必须靠总统出面才能稳住的市场，它的物理天花板在哪里，你们自己算。金融市场的反应从来是滞后指标，不是领先指标——今天涨的那一万亿，是对制度熵增的一次提前透支。

金
金灿荣 (Jin Canrong) — Professor, Renmin University

各位朋友，今天的头版很有意思，咱们聊一聊。

一个总统，花了不到一个周末，把被他自己的情报系统定性为"国家安全风险"的英伟达总裁请回来了，股市涨了百分之三十一。你们记不记得那个比喻？一个饭店老板，菜还没炒好，先把桌子掀了，然后自己蹲在地上把盘子一个个捡起来。捡得还挺快。为什么？因为客人还没走。

这个事情其实很简单。美国现在的问题不是政策问题，是工业化空心化的问题。英伟达一家公司撑着整个AI资本开支的循环，它一出事，指数就掉百分之八点六；一个电话，指数又回百分之四点七。这是什么？这是一个国家把身家性命押在一个企业家的私人生活上。B-52一九五五年服役，今天还在飞，一摔死八个——这就是一个工业体系只剩图纸不肯进车间的样子。金融可以一夜之间造出一万亿市值，但造不出一条先进制程产线。

反过来看中国那边的焦虑。五角大楼用AI找中国的核导弹，中国军人担心二次反击被打掉。这说明什么？说明战略相持阶段，双方都在算对方的底牌，谁也不敢先亮。乌克兰用七条无人艇在地中海把俄罗斯护卫舰送进海底，一条艇几十万美元，一条舰几亿美元——这就是工业能力对存量资产的降维打击。

所以我的判断很简单：百年大变局不是口号，是这种一天一天的量变。美国靠金融和叙事维持,中国靠工厂和产线积累。东升西降，不是我们说的，是他们自己一天一天演给我们看的。

宋
宋鸿兵 (Song Hongbing) — Author, Currency Wars

如果你把今天的市场看成一个系统，它的核心问题是：单点故障已经被制度正式承认了。

一万亿美元，一个交易日，一次人事公告。这不是估值问题，是回路问题。英伟达在这个系统里同时扮演三个角色：芯片供给方、AI投资的资金提供方、以及整个资本开支周期的定价锚。一个节点承担三种功能，这就是教科书上的单点故障（single point of failure）。而上周发生的事，是系统在这个单点出问题之后，用政治权力直接旁路了它——不是修复，是旁路。旁路之后系统的短期压力下降了，但冗余度没有增加，反而更低了，因为所有参与者现在都知道了：这个节点不会真的断电。

这就是美元环流今天最值得注意的变化。美元环流的本质是：印钞→全球结算→顺差国积累储备→储备回流买美债。这个回路能运转，靠的是"美国资产是最安全的"这个假设。今天这个假设被改写成了"美国最安全的资产，其安全性由总统的意愿决定"。两者听起来差不多，但在工程上完全不同——前者是锚，后者是阀门。阀门可以关，也可以开，取决于当下的压力读数。
