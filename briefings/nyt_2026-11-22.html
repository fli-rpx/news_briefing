Cluster 1: Geopolitics & International Affairs

Thematic Hook

The postwar bargain — American security for allied compliance — has been replaced by a transaction in which the suppliers have changed sides, and everyone is pricing their own exposure.

Top Drug Kingpin Is Captured by Mexican Army as Gunfire Erupts (#5)

The capture of Fausto Isidro Meza Flores — known as El Chapo Isidro, one of the F.B.I.'s ten most wanted — was executed by the Mexican Army on American intelligence, at a cost of four Mexican soldiers and seven suspected cartel members, with no civilians harmed. The DEA called it a "significant blow" and the U.S. ambassador cited "the exchange of strategic intelligence between the United States and Mexico." This is the model Washington prefers everywhere now: violence outsourced to a partner that carries the legitimacy, and the bill paid in intelligence rather than troops.

Europe Needs to Save Itself (#18)

Timothy Garton Ash's counsel to European capitals, after cheering the American midterms, is the most unsentimental thing in the paper: act as if "an even worse version of Mr. Trump is likely to enter the White House on Jan. 20, 2029." He names the trap precisely — the temptation to exhale with "Ah, the United States is coming back, after all." European leaders know they must redirect serious spending to defense, and know that aging populations, large welfare states, high debt and rising nationalist parties may end their governments if they do. That is not a policy dilemma. It is a description of a continent choosing between two ways of losing.

Can a Country Be Guilty of Genocide? Finally, the Answer Is Yes. (#19)

The International Court of Justice found Myanmar responsible for genocide against the Rohingya — the first time a state, not an individual, has been so judged. Philippe Sands, who has spent three decades in this field, says he has never heard a judgment read for nearly three hours, and the 9–6 split matters: six judges held that Myanmar committed war crimes and human rights violations but not genocide. The ruling is a genuine advance for the rule of law and a reminder that the term remains contested at the exact moment it acquires its first state-shaped body.

U.N. Fails to Regulate Lethal Autonomous Weapons, but Plans to Try Again (#20)

128 countries spent a week in Geneva and produced a two-year extension of the conversation. The United States, Russia and Israel refused binding restrictions; Ukraine, which already fields partly autonomous drones, argued for meaningful human control. The detail that should be read twice is that China was the only major power willing to accept a binding protocol — a position that costs Beijing little now and constrains Washington's advantage later.

On the Trail With Spain's Prime Minister, as He Tries to Pull Out a Win (#21)

Pedro Sánchez, Europe's most prominent left-wing leader, is polling to lose on Nov. 29 after eight years. "I always use elbows," he says, and "that is good for me... they underestimate me." Abroad he is a hero of the global left for resisting Trump, welcoming migrants and calling Gaza genocide. At home, Spain's growth and renewable build-out have not converted into affection. He is the clearest test case in Europe of whether a leader can survive being more popular outside his own country than inside it.

Voters Are Punishing Autocrats. But Many Still Embrace Them. Why? (#22)

Damien Cave reports on research linking authoritarian support to a belief that the world is inherently hierarchical — countries, people, even animals scaled worst to best — and that for those who move from seeing hierarchy to demanding its enforcement, a strongman becomes logical. "It reframes where authoritarianism is coming from," says Jer Clifton of Penn. The finding is unsettling for the post-midterm mood: elections can punish autocrats without curing the appetite that produces them.

Pentagon Issued Memo Limiting 'Social Activities' With Canadian Military (#23)

A Defense Department memo discourages informal social contact between American and Canadian officers, according to Senator Mike Rounds, while NORAD continues to function. Rounds frames it as an emphasis on "getting some agreements on military purchases." This is friction manufactured at the one place where friction costs nothing to impose and everything to bear: the informal trust that makes joint watchkeeping work.

Cluster 2: US Politics & Domestic Policy

Thematic Hook

The wave and the quorum are the same argument conducted in opposite directions — one says a majority is sacred, the other says an improperly assembled majority was never a majority at all.

A 'Catastrophic Failure': Watchdog Officials Find Cascade of Problems With ICE Training (#4)

An unreleased 19-page draft inspector-general report finds that DHS loosened training standards to deploy 10,000 new immigration officers fast. More than 3,500 recruits with prior law enforcement experience trained virtually, some finishing in under a day; testing was opened to unlimited retakes, and one officer passed on the 32nd attempt. A recruit instructor's phrase, quoted in the draft, is the summary: "catastrophic failure." Secretary Mullin restored the stricter standards in July, but the report says ICE "has not fully addressed training gaps born of the reductions and revisions." You can restore a rule; you cannot restore a deployed officer's competence.

Obama Tells Incoming House Democrats to Keep Up Outreach in Red Areas (#12)

In a 75-minute session at the Folger Shakespeare Library, Obama told the incoming class — democratic socialists and Midwestern centrists in the same room — that the country is in a moment of "generational change," and that "the diversity of this class is its power," regional and ideological as much as racial. He also praised Hakeem Jeffries, nominated that day to be the first Black speaker. The advice amounts to a warning: a majority built on a wave has to keep visiting the districts it did not win.

Democrats Will Wield Enormous Power in Statehouses. What Will They Do? (#13)

Six new trifectas — Arizona, Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Nevada, Michigan, Minnesota — and majorities in a dozen new chambers, on at least 530 flipped seats. "It is hard to overstate how massive of a political earthquake this was," says Arizona's incoming House speaker, who at 33 was not alive the last time Democrats held any Arizona chamber. The agenda in WI, PA and NV is concrete and fast: minimum wage, union recognition, paid family leave. The statehouses, not Washington, are where the wave becomes law.

'It's Easier Said Than Done': 3 Opinion Writers on How to Check Trump (#14)

David French calls the Iran war "perhaps the most unpopular war in modern American history" — begun without Congress's consent and pursued against public will. The symposium's real subject is whether a large Democratic majority will do the constitutionally ambitious thing and reclaim the war power, or settle for hearings. French's argument is that Congress has an opening it may not get again.

Sarah McBride, Barred From Capitol Bathrooms, Wins Top Post (#15)

Two years to the day after Republicans moved to ban her from women's bathrooms, House Democrats elected McBride a co-chair of the Democratic Policy and Communications Committee — the No. 8 leadership post — making her a first-term lawmaker with a seat in the speaker's weekly strategy meetings. The bathroom ban expires next year, and several of her loudest antagonists will be gone. Her rise is the clearest cultural marker of the transfer of power.

A Dairy Farmer Ran for Office to Prove a Point. He Accidentally Won. (#16)

Nathan Miller, a Green-leaning independent who registered as a Democrat to run for county assessor in northwest Arkansas, spent $225, did not campaign, and won while milking a cow. "That doesn't seem like any kind of democracy," he said of unopposed Republicans. His win is the granular proof of the wave's depth: down-ballot races normally insulated from national trends flipped on ruby-red terrain.

Federal Court Halts Pregnant Workers' Protection Law (#17)

A divided Fifth Circuit ruled 9–8 that the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act was never constitutionally enacted, because the pandemic-era proxy votes Congress used to establish a quorum do not count. "We conclude that the Act's passage was unconstitutional because the Quorum Clause does not allow proxies to be included when establishing a quorum." The ruling voids accommodations — bathroom and water breaks, relief from heavy lifting, time off for prenatal appointments — for roughly 20 million women in Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi, and is expected to reach the Supreme Court. The doctrine does not stop at this statute: it implicates every law passed under proxy voting, including the $1.7 trillion vehicle that carried it.

This Autocratic Tactic Is on the Rise (#28)

The editorial board identifies a specific move — using state power to license, intimidate and impoverish journalists — and treats its spread as the leading indicator of democratic backsliding, not a side effect of it. Read alongside the quorum ruling, the point sharpens: institutions do not fall all at once, they fall through procedural precedent.

Times Opinion convenes its writers on how a party with the largest House majority in decades, six new trifectas and 530+ new state legislators should sequence its first hundred days — with the unstated problem that the floor beneath those majorities is being challenged in court.

Thematic Hook

Threats, Insults, Settlements: How Jensen Huang Made Humiliation Routine (#1)

The Times' investigation, based on more than a dozen current and former employees and hundreds of pages of court records spanning twenty years, documents a management doctrine built on degradation: an executive told his mother should have aborted him; a subordinate told his arms would be ripped off and used to beat him to death; another told his skull would be opened and urinated into. "I'd rather torture you into greatness, because I believe in you," Huang said. One episode produced a confidential settlement with an executive who said he "terrorized" staff. The board did not act for two decades, because the stock went up.

Nvidia Said to Face S.E.C. Probe Into Its Accounting Practices (#2)

The SEC is investigating Nvidia's accounting, per two people briefed. The thread that matters: after the Times reported on Nvidia's relationship with Singapore-based Megaspeed — where the executive at the center of the affair worked, and which Commerce was investigating for allegedly smuggling AI chips into China — Nvidia switched from reporting sales by "billing location" to "customers' headquarters," and Singapore disappeared from its disclosures. Nothing about the chips moved. The map did.

Stocks Often Prosper After an Abrupt C.E.O. Exit. Is Nvidia Different? (#3)

More than $2 trillion of market value has vanished since late October — roughly the combined worth of JPMorgan, Exxon, UnitedHealthcare and Netflix — and shares closed down 14 percent on Friday. Huang said Tuesday he would be "honored to answer these calls and return as C.E.O."; the board made clear it would not have him. Jeff Sommer's historical finding is that mature companies usually recover from founder exits once a permanent successor is installed. Nvidia's complication is that its founder was also a channel to Beijing.

Japan Attempts to Revive an American Industrial Icon (#9)

Nippon Steel's $14.9 billion purchase of U.S. Steel — approved in 2025 after more than a year of opposition, including from the Biden administration — has put Japanese engineers inside Andrew Carnegie's 1875 Edgar Thomson mill, clearing land for a rolling mill five football fields long and moving Japanese process methods into the Mon Valley. "The excitement level is the highest I've seen it here in my 27 years," says a U.S. Steel director. The buyer is doing the industrial policy the seller would not.

Texas Republicans Gave Elon Musk $1.7 Billion, Secrecy and Access. Now What? (#10)

Musk asked Greg Abbott on a Valentine's Day call in 2024 whether Texas would back his companies if he reincorporated there; Abbott said "one hundred percent." A Times analysis counts at least $1.7 billion in grants, tax breaks and other incentives since — against roughly $500 million Musk spent on Republicans nationwide. Then Texas voted out many of his allies, including Abbott. Musk's exposure is now physical: seven facilities, at least 20,000 acres, a spaceport. He cannot decamp the way he left California.

Debt Problems of the Richest Countries Are Trickling Down to the Poorest (#24)

Rich-world debt is transmitting to developing economies through higher borrowing costs, thinner aid and slower growth, with refinancing costs climbing as concessional finance shrinks — the opposite of the post-pandemic assumption that poorer economies had decoupled from rich-world fiscal trouble.

Cluster 4: Culture & Society

Thematic Hook

Three arguments about authorship — a tile game redefined by outsiders, taste redefined by machines, and three lives that settled the question by simply outlasting it.

America Loves Mahjong. Chinese Americans Hardly Recognize It. (#11)

322 contestants entered a Chinese mahjong tournament in Monterey Park, whittled to 50 at the final tables, playing for $3,000. Around them, American mahjong — different tiles, different rules, a multimillion-dollar industry — has become the country's default version of the game, with Yelp searches for mahjong clubs up more than 4,000 percent in a year. "Playing mahjong has always been a moment for us to connect over Lunar New Year," says Steven Hoang, 32. "It feels like American mahjong doesn't really pay tribute to that history." The dispute is about commerce and credit, and about who gets to define a tradition after it has been popularized by people outside it.

The Internet Broke Our Taste. Will A.I. Destroy It for Good? (#8)

Jonah Weiner's magazine essay asks the question that follows from slop capitalism and vibe-crafted culture: whether machines can be said to have taste at all, and what is left of ours if they can fake it convincingly. Set beside the same week's AI business news — an Anthropic IPO, a claimed Riemann proof, "rogue agent episodes" — the cultural question stops being aesthetic. If generated competence is indistinguishable from earned competence, the only remaining scarce good is the ability to tell.

Obituaries: Marsha Linehan, Kevin Bleyer, Olivier Mourgue (#29)

Linehan built dialectical behavior therapy for borderline personality disorder and then disclosed that she had the diagnosis herself; Bleyer wrote the speech that roasted Trump at the 2011 correspondents' dinner and has died at 54; Mourgue designed the "Djinn" chairs that floated through "2001: A Space Odyssey." Three different kinds of authorship, all of which outlived their authors without needing a board's permission.

Anthropic Mathematician Claims to Have Solved Math's Most Famous Problem (#6)

Levent Alpoge posted a four-page note claiming a proof of the Riemann hypothesis produced with Claude, offering no paper and no peer review, only a link to code he says verifies the result. I Spent a Year Talking to Anthropic Employees. Here's What They Believe. (#7)

Trump Administration Says Army Can Blow Up Lead-Laced Explosives (#25)

The Justice Department argues the Army may detonate 68 drums — nearly 9,700 pounds — of lead azide in southeast Iowa as soon as Monday, potentially sending a lead-dust plume over homes and farms, despite a federal judge's temporary block on the permit. The Army says the material is too unstable to move and that sub-freezing temperatures make it worse; environmental groups are seeking an emergency stay. A court order, an agency assertion of inherent authority and a decaying explosive are now in a three-way race.

Judge Approves $7 Billion Settlement Over Claims That Roundup Caused Cancer (#26)

A Missouri court gave final approval to a $7.25 billion nationwide settlement ending tens of thousands of suits claiming Roundup caused non-Hodgkin lymphoma, paid over 21 years with awards of $10,000 to $165,000 graded by severity, age and occupation. Judge Timothy Boyer called the awards "fair and adequate"; Bayer's chief executive called it a "decisive milestone." Combined with this summer's Supreme Court ruling on labeling, a decade of litigation is converted into a long-dated annuity.

New evidence opens the door to prescribing GLP-1 drugs to young children, and pediatricians are split — over effects on growth and development, and over whether a system already stretched by adult demand can absorb pediatric use.
