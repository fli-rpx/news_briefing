WSJ DAILY BRIEFING

Wednesday, November 11, 2026

Executive Summary

  • The Fed's problem changed shape overnight. For two years the question was when the cutting cycle would resume.
  • Wednesday's front page answers a different one: how many hikes are coming.
  • A third consecutive month of firm underlying inflation, combined with hawkish Fed commentary, has put a December increase back in play with the 10-year at 4.990%, the dollar at a four-week high and the yen at a two-month low despite a record single-day Japanese intervention in July.

The AI complex split into a hardware trade and a credibility trade. The Nasdaq rose 1.29% to 25,389.04 while the Dow fell 0.05% to 52,208.10 — a 134-basis-point spread inside one session. CoreWeave's earnings and a $133 billion backlog lifted Sandisk (+11.79%), Micron (+8.50%) and AMD (+3.59%). In the same hours, Nvidia retained Wachtell Lipton to investigate whether the company was the target of foreign espionage, OpenAI reported $10 billion in revenue against continuing heavy losses, and SoftBank wrote down a turbine reservation large enough to power six Hoover Dams.

Washington changed hands before the news did. Democrats won Congress on November 3 and take over in January. Pete Hegseth survived a House impeachment effort this week, but the story is explicit that Democrats are expected to revive it. The more consequential shift is at the Fed: Nick Timiraos reports that a Democratic Senate is now the broadest check yet on the president's efforts to control the central bank.

The war's material tail is mispriced. OPEC output is back near prewar levels, yet crude traded at $87.45 (+1.64%) and Brent recovered above $92. Houthi strikes hit an iconic Saudi tower, tankers carrying Russian diesel for the U.S. shifted course for want of buyers, and Poland's Orlen cancelled a Qatari LNG contract outright.

The trust deficit is now a market input. A $26 million cyber heist in Cranbury, New Jersey went undisclosed until after the election. A 41-year-old U.S. Army soldier was arrested in Britain on allegations of spying for Russia from inside a NATO command. SEC enforcement penalties fell to about $1 billion, an 18-year low.

Cluster 1: Geopolitics & Defense

1.1 The Nvidia story has acquired an intelligence dimension

The Nvidia story crossed a threshold this week. "Law Firm Wachtell Lipton to Lead Nvidia Internal Investigation" reports that the elite New York firm will probe whether the technology company was the target of a foreign espionage effort — a mandate about state actors, not about an executive's private conduct. No source in the file states that the probe arises from the Huang affair; the two threads are adjacent, not linked. That framing matters because it changes what the investigation can produce: a security finding rather than an HR finding.

The political response has already moved. "Scandal Revives Push for Chip Restrictions on Capitol Hill" and the morning risk file's "Risk Report: Hackers Are Stealing AI Computing Power" describe two distinct vectors — export-control tightening and the theft of compute itself. Holman W. Jenkins Jr. argues in "China's Jensen Huang Caper" that "before going off half-cocked, we ought to ask how Beijing thinks it benefits," a caution that assumes a rational adversary rather than a lucky one. The editorial board is less patient: "Nvidia Gets a Chinese Honeypot Lesson".

"The Morning Risk Report: How Hackers Steal Millions in AI Computing Power" (Richard Vanderford) makes the second vector explicit: the compute itself is the target, and the theft is measured in millions. Taiwan is the quiet casualty. "Jensen Huang Affair Strains Taiwan's Devotion to Its Hometown Hero" (Austin Ramzy) describes a birthplace whose national identity is partly invested in one executive. "Resilience of the Global AI Boom Is Being Tested" and "Nvidia Scandal Tests the Resilience of the Global AI Boom" (Tim Higgins) ask the strategic question directly: Huang is "the biggest cheerleader of the 'next industrial revolution'" — could it carry on without him? "The Nvidia Affair Is Terrible Timing for Corporate America" (Greg Ip) answers from the governance side.

Calibrated view: 65% probability that Nvidia's internal investigation produces a disclosure within 90 days that forces a board-level governance change — a separation of the CEO from an external-facing role — but no criminal referral against the company — (rationale: Wachtell's mandate as reported is whether the company was an espionage target, and "AI Is Testing the Limits of Corporate Governance" frames the issue as process, not liability).

1.2 Iran: the war that will not conclude

"Pete Hegseth Survives House Impeachment Effort" carries the day's largest comment count — 1,370 — and a forward-looking clause: GOP leaders blocked the move, and "House Democrats are expected to revive the effort when they take control of Congress in January." Damian Paletta's "House GOP Saves Hegseth—for Now" adds that the House GOP also picked Jim Jordan to lead the conference.

On the ground, the editorial board's "Iran Doesn't Think the War Is Over" notes the U.S. again moved aircraft from a British air base after threats, and "The Hegseth Impeachment Spectacle" treats the House vote as political theater rather than accountability. Tim Sheehy's "If You Think War Is Unpopular, Wait Till You See Defeat" invokes Truman to argue that history vindicates politically costly decisions. "Iconic Saudi Tower Hit as Yemen's Houthis Target Riyadh" and "To Cross the Red Sea, Shippers Email Houthi Rebels. Read Their Inbox." describe a shipping lane governed by correspondence with a militia.

The diplomatic track is running on a separate clock. "U.S. Overture to Russia on European Security Sparks Alarm" reports that U.S. officials want Ukraine ceasefire work to proceed alongside talks on Europe's future security architecture — a linkage that alarms allies because it prices their arrangement into a settlement they do not control. "Ten Months of War to Gain 46 Square Miles: How Russia Is Failing on the Battlefield" quantifies the stalemate; "A Soldier Cut Off His Own Foot to Survive. Then Came a Daring Drone Rescue." renders its human cost.

One second-order effect deserves attention: "Global CO2 Emissions to Fall as Iran War Cuts Fossil-Fuel Use" reports that a consortium of climate scientists now expects a decline driven by reduced fossil consumption — an outcome no climate policy produced, delivered instead by a supply shock.

Calibrated view: 70% probability that a revived Hegseth removal or censure effort is formally filed in the new Congress within its first 100 days — (rationale: the front-page story states the expectation directly, and Paletta's column treats "for now" as the operative phrase).

1.3 The spy inside the command structure

"Senior U.S. Soldier Is Arrested in U.K. Over Allegations of Spying for Russia" is the clearest institutional failure of the day: a 41-year-old assigned to a NATO rapid reaction command structure in the U.K., arrested on allegations of spying for an adversary. "U.K. Police Arrested a U.S. Army Soldier on Allegations of Spying for Russia" is the What's News version, bundling it with rate anxiety and Anthropic's founder windfall.

Alongside it, "U.K.'s Worst-Kept Secret: Who Was the Celebrity Accused of Preying on Teens" examines the David Walliams case — allegations he denies — as a study in Britain's libel regime and its limits on press freedom. Two very different stories about the same country's information controls.

Cluster 2: Markets & Macroeconomy

2.1 The repricing: from cuts to hikes

"One More Rate Hike Is Looking Like the Beginning, Not the End" (Emma Tucker's 10-Point) states the new regime in its headline; the same newsletter carries the spy arrest and the Cranbury cyber heist as separate items alongside it. "Fed Forced to Confront Case for Bigger and Faster Rate Rises" (Nick Timiraos and Matt Grossman) is the day's most important story. "A third straight month of firm underlying inflation reopens the question of how far and how fast the central bank must go." "Broad Price Pressures Firm Up Case for Fed Rate Increase in December" (Matt Grossman) supplies the detail: consumer prices "climbed notably again in October from the previous month, extending a stretch of stubborn inflation."

Two supporting prints matter. "Solid Jobs Report Leaves Inflation, Markets to Settle Fed's December Call" notes recent data point toward another increase, with the caveat that "a more sustained AI selloff could argue against one." And "Hiring Defied Expectations With 165,000 Jobs Added in October" confirms the labor market is not cooperating with the disinflation story.

The international transmission is already visible. "Bank of England Raises Rates for First Time Since Start of Iran War" attributes the move to energy prices lifted by the conflict — though those prices have since fallen on hopes the midterms produce an end to it. "Asian Bond Yields Rise on Growing Expectations for Fed Rate Hike" (Sherry Qin and Kimberley Kao) confirms the export of U.S. tightening.

Calibrated view: 75% probability the Fed raises its target range at the December meeting — (rationale: three consecutive firm CPI months, hawkish official commentary as reported by Timiraos, a solid 165,000-job print, and a 10-year already at 4.990%).

2.2 Rates, currencies, and the limits of intervention

The tape shows a narrow move and a loud narrative. The 10-year closed Tuesday at 4.990%, down 7/32 in price, or roughly 2.5 basis points higher in yield, with Treasurys shut for the holiday. "U.S. Treasurys Pause on Market Holiday After Inflation-Fueled Selloff" (Emese Bartha) and "Eurozone Bond Yields Little Changed" both note the absence of a Treasury print to follow.

The dollar did the work instead. "Dollar's Strength Could Persist on U.S. Rate-Rise Prospects" reports a four-week high against a basket of currencies, with ING expecting near-term firmness. "Yen Slumps as Fed Rate-Hike Bets Boost Dollar" puts the yen at two-month lows — and "Japan Spent Record $50 Billion in Single Day to Bolster Yen" records that Tokyo burned a record single-day sum in July, including rare joint action with the U.S., and still lost the level. Intervention without a rate differential is a donation.

"For Trump's Fed Ambitions, a Democratic Senate Poses the Biggest Hurdle Yet" (Timiraos) is the political leg of the same trade: a Democratic majority would be "the broadest check yet" on presidential influence over the central bank.

Calibrated view: 70% probability the 10-year trades above 5.00% before year-end — (rationale: 4.990% is 1bp from the level, the hike path is repricing toward December, and the run-in is shortened by the holiday, and "Fed Speakers, Tech Earnings: What to Watch the Rest of the Week" flags a dense calendar).

2.4 Commodities: a freight premium, not scarcity

"OPEC's Oil Production Nears Prewar Levels on Saudi Rebound" (Giulia Petroni) reports October output returned toward prewar levels via alternative pipelines. If supply is restored, why is crude at $87.45 (+1.64%)? "Oil Prices Rebound as Middle East Supply Risks Persist" (Farhan Rafid) answers: Brent recovered above $92 "as renewed strikes tempered expectations of a diplomatic resolution." "Crude Futures Pick Up After Heavy Selling" notes futures had slid to two-month lows on peace optimism first. The residual is at least a $4.55 spread between Brent and the U.S. crude benchmark — a risk premium priced per barrel delivered, not per barrel produced.

"Trump's Plan to Import Russian Diesel Stalls on the High Seas" (Alexander Osipovich, Rebecca Feng, Collin Eaton) is the cleanest example of sanctions friction: tankers bound for the U.S. "shifted course following difficulties finding buyers." "Poland's Orlen Cancels Qatar LNG Supply Deal After Months of Force Majeure" marks the first known cancellation with QatarEnergy since the war began. "U.S. Natural Gas Futures Continue to Climb" cites cooler forecasts, high LNG feedgas and lower production.

Gold at 4,023.80 (-0.16%) against a dollar up 0.48% is a telling non-move: no one is buying the hedge the headlines imply they need. "Gold's Outlook Remains Compelling in Long Term" has Julius Baer arguing inflationary pressure should ease next year, letting the Fed be less aggressive than feared.

Cluster 3: Business & Tech Strategy

3.1 The capital-intensity problem nobody wants to name

Set the numbers side by side. CoreWeave's backlog is $133 billion; OpenAI's revenue is $10 billion. The source does not break the backlog down by customer, but a commitment book of that size is only intelligible if a handful of AI labs are underwriting it — and the largest such counterparty books an order of magnitude less revenue than the commitments it would be carrying. "CoreWeave Finds a Workaround to Capital Constraints—for Now" is the honest headline of the week: the workaround is a bridge, and bridges need re-underwriting.

SoftBank supplies the failure case. "Masayoshi Son's Huge Bet on Electrical Turbines Backfires" reports the company reserved enough gas turbines "to generate the power of six Hoover Dams," costing billions in losses. The loss reads as a timing error — not a demand error — on a permitting and interconnection constraint that capital cannot clear.

"AI Is Testing the Limits of Corporate Governance" (Steven Rosenbush) connects the financing structure to the oversight structure, and "Is AI Taking White-Collar Jobs? What the Latest Numbers Tell Us" supplies the labor-demand question underneath the capex. "About Your Next Board Meeting" (Erle Norton) is blunt about what boards will be asked.

Calibrated view: 55% probability CoreWeave returns to public or private capital markets for additional financing within six months — (rationale: the reported workaround is explicitly temporary, the backlog requires buildout ahead of receipts, and "Why CoreWeave Matters So Much for the AI Trade" ties its funding access to the whole complex).

3.2 Reshoring meets arithmetic

"An American Furniture Maker Tried to Bring Jobs Back Home. It Didn't Work." (Stu Woo, 974 comments) is the most consequential business story on the page. The Krinskys moved manufacturing back from Vietnam to stay ahead of shifting trade policy and "the numbers didn't pencil out." Every tariff argument in Washington now has a counterexample with a balance sheet attached.

Adjacent industrial stories point the same way. "Can Hotels, Libraries and Apartment Towers Absorb America's Timber Glut?" (Ryan Dezember) proposes mass timber as a demand sink for a domestic surplus. "Toyota's Scion Brand Returns With Japan's Forbidden-Fruit Minitrucks" is an automaker hedging against its own electrification bet. "Nissan, Back in Red, Cuts Sales View on China Slump" shows the cost of losing the Chinese market. "Why High Beef Prices Are Here to Stay", "I Have a Beef With the Price of Steak" (Matthew Hennessey) and the shrinking cattle herd describe food inflation with a multi-year supply lag. "Masayoshi Son's Huge Bet on Electrical Turbines Backfires" again — the same capital-intensity lesson in a different sector.

翟东升——体系/结构视角

# 翟东升观察：2026年11月11日

美联储的难题一夜之间换了形状。两年来的问题是降息何时重启，今天的问题是要加几次。十年期美债4.990%，美元四周新高，日元两月新低——而东京此前曾单日投入创纪录的五百亿美元、还罕见地拉着美方联合干预，依然没守住那个点位。底层逻辑是：不改变利差的干预，本质是一次单向捐款。

但这不是货币政策的问题，这是主权信用的定价问题。市场用加息预期给美元加分，说明在利差层面美元仍是稀缺抵押品；可同一份数据里，黄金4,023.80几乎纹丝不动——真正该避险的时候，没人买避险资产。这说明美元的强是相对的强，不是绝对的强：它强在欧洲与日本的更弱，强在平行体系尚未建成。这是结构性两难的现行版本——既不能大幅降息，债务付息与AI资本开支都靠低成本资金续命；也不能真正紧缩到位。

值得注意的是政治的介入。民主党拿下参议院，被描述为对总统控制央行"迄今最广的制约"。我的判断是，这不触及债务结构那道物理天花板，只是把铸币权的争夺从行政分支挪到立法分支——制度熵增，不是制度修复。

AI那一侧更直白：CoreWeave积压订单1,330亿，OpenAI收入100亿。订单是承诺，收入是现金，中间隔着资本市场"暂时"的配合。一个边际回报率趋零的行业靠融资结构撑估值，和美元靠利差撑汇率，是同一逻辑的两层皮。真正脆弱的从来不是价格，是支撑价格的那套制度可信度。

金灿荣——美国政治/战略相持视角

# 金灿荣观察：2026年11月11日

各位朋友，今天这份报纸，我一看就乐了——美国现在是两头使劲，可惜一头使在城墙上，一头使在自己脚上。

先说芯片。英伟达请了华尔街最贵的律师，去查自己"是不是被外国间谍盯上了"，国会那边同步又要把芯片限制收紧。这个事情其实很简单：一个瓜摊老板，发现隔壁开始自己种瓜了，第一反应不是改良品种，是翻账本查伙计。这就是守成心态。技术封锁这个东西，越到后面越像堵水，你堵一处，它渗三处。我们这边呢？阿里巴巴把芯片业务拿出来上市——家底厚了，才敢摆到台面上。战略相持阶段讲"互有攻守"，就是这么个味道。

再看那条家具厂的新闻，一家人从越南把厂子搬回美国，最后"算不过账"。这是今天最有分量的故事，可惜很多人当商业版花絮看。关税不是工厂，补贴也不是工厂。我讲过多少次，霸权不是靠嘴硬维持的，是靠工厂里的机器、实验室里的技术和老百姓的饭碗撑起来的。

AI那块也有意思：一边是一千三百三十亿美元的订单，一边是全行业最大的一家AI公司一年收入才一百亿。这不是工业能力，这是账本上的算术。工业这个东西，得有人拧螺丝，得有人守夜班，估值守不出来。

最后中期选举，共和党输得很难看，原因一句话——物价涨得比工资快。美国的问题从来不是本事不够，是共识不够：一个饭店老板，菜还没炒好就先掀桌子，客人自然就走了。对外还没打赢，对内先散了架。

所以2026年11月11日这一天，我的判断还是那六个字：战略相持，东升西降。

宋鸿兵——货币/债务/周期视角

# 宋鸿兵观察：2026年11月11日

如果你把美元环流看作一台机器，今天的读数只说明一件事：主阀门正在反向拧紧。

十月CPI连续第三个月顽固，十年期停在4.990%，美元指数升至四周高点——市场把十二月的问题从"降不降"改写成"加多少"。5%不是心理关口，而是债务滚动成本的反馈回路：一旦越过，付息压力自动追加发债，发债再推高收益率，这个回路不需要任何人推动就会自己转起来。

但今天最值得记下的不是美元强，而是日元弱。东京在七月单日抛出创纪录的500亿美元，还拉上美国联合干预，仍然丢掉了那个价位。这印证一条铁律：没有利差支撑的干预，不是防守，是捐款。汇率是一国货币的价格，价格由环流方向决定，不由央行意志决定。日本烧掉的是储备，想保住的是面子。

黄金停在4,023.80，美元涨0.48%，它几乎不动。战争、间谍调查、算力被窃，这些头条本该喂饱避险盘，可是没有。这不是黄金失效，而是市场仍在相信美元的清算能力——美元环流的断裂，还没有走到被定价的那一步。

真正的新战线在结算层：纳斯达克背景的Payward把两百多只欧洲股票做成代币。清算与结算一旦迁出中央银行的轨道，定价权就跟着迁移。这是金融高边疆上一次静默的登陆，比关税更值得警惕。
